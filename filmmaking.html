Film & Video — an open studio

Photography's moving sibling: stories told in time instead of instants. Film and video braid every other wing of this library — image, sound, writing, performance, digital craft — into the most collaborative art that exists. This page covers the crafts, the technique groups, the vocabulary, the masters, a short history, a first year, and the professional paths that actually pay.

The field in one page

A film is a photograph that lasts, a piece of music you can see, an argument staged like theater, and a building project run on a schedule. Nobody masters all of it; everybody learns to speak everyone else's language. The core skill underneath every specialty is the same: deciding what the audience sees, when, and in what order.

Why people stay

Nothing else in this library ends with a room full of strangers laughing, gasping, or sitting silently through credits. Film rewards generalists — the editor who can hear, the writer who can frame, the director who knows what a budget means.

The honest difficulty

It is the most expensive craft per minute to practice badly. The cure is scale: make things small enough to finish. A finished one-minute short teaches more than an abandoned feature — see the scope-tiny rule from the digital crafts, imported here wholesale.

Technique groups

Pre-production — where films are actually made

Script breakdown
Read the script as a list of requirements: locations, cast, props, wardrobe, effects. Every line item is money and minutes.
Shot list & storyboards
Decide coverage on paper, not on the clock: wide, mediums, close-ups, the inserts you'll be grateful for in the edit. Storyboard the sequences where timing is the meaning.
The schedule
Shoot in location order, not story order; group by light and cast. Amateurs schedule optimistically, professionals schedule for weather.
Casting as authorship
A great casting choice rewrites half the dialogue for free; a wrong one makes every scene push uphill.
The rehearsal you can afford
Table reads cost sandwiches and expose every line that nobody can actually say.

Camera & coverage

Coverage discipline
Master for geography, singles for performance, inserts for detail, a safety take for the gods. You cannot re-shoot the performance you didn't record.
The 180-degree rule
Keep the camera on one side of the axis between two characters or the audience's map of the room breaks. Break it on purpose, never by accident.
Motivated movement
Dolly, pan, and handheld are punctuation, not wallpaper — every camera move should have a reason a skeptical editor could state out loud.
Light for the story
The five portrait patterns scale up to scenes; motivated sources (windows, lamps) keep fiction believing in itself.
Shot size as grammar
Wide says where, medium says who, close-up says what it costs. The cut between sizes is a sentence.

Edit & rhythm

Assembly → rough → fine
First pass for structure, second for scene rhythm, third for frame-level polish. Reordering the assembly is free; re-shooting is not.
Cut on action
Hide the cut inside movement; the eye follows the motion and forgives the splice.
J and L cuts
Audio leading or trailing picture — the difference between a scene that flows and a scene that clanks.
Kill your darlings, again
The beautiful shot that stops the story goes. The editor's culling discipline from stills applies frame-for-frame.
Temp music trap
Score carved to a temp track can only ever be that temp track; cut rhythm to the scene, then let music find it.

Sound & finishing

Room tone
Thirty seconds of silence at every location — the patch material that saves dialogue editing. Free, and unbuyable later.
Dialogue is king
The mix serves intelligibility first; an audience will forgive a noisy image before an inaudible line.
Foley & ambience
Footsteps, cloth, doors, and the room's breathing — the layer that makes cut images feel continuous.
The grade
Color as final photography: matching shots, then shaping mood — the same logic as grading stills, with continuity added.
Loudness, not loudness
Delivery specs exist; a mix that crushes everything at maximum is exhausting, not impressive.

Vocabulary

Shot sizes
Wide/establishing, medium, close-up, extreme close-up — the sentence lengths of film grammar.
Coverage
The set of shots that makes a scene editable.
Axis / 180° rule
The invisible line that keeps screen direction consistent in a scene.
Cutaway
A shot of something else — hands, a clock, a listener — that lets the editor bend time.
Mise-en-scène
Everything in front of the camera: staging, design, light, costume — the frame's contents as meaning.
Montage
Meaning built between shots by juxtaposition; also, a sequence compressing time.
Dolly / pan / tilt
Camera moves: travels, swivels horizontally, tips vertically.
B-roll
Supporting imagery cut over narration or interviews — documentary's connective tissue.
Foley
Everyday sound effects performed in sync to picture.
Diegetic sound
Sound whose source exists inside the story world (the radio on screen) versus score, which doesn't.
J / L cut
Audio arriving before or after its picture — the seam-hiding cuts of polished editing.
Room tone
A location's recorded silence; the editor's mortar.
Fps
Frames per second — 24 the cinematic default, higher for slow motion.
Aspect ratio
Frame shape — 16:9 for screens, 2.39:1 for scope widescreen, 4:3 the classical box.
Color grade
The post-production pass that shapes color and tone across the whole piece.
Call sheet
Tomorrow's plan: who, where, when, what scenes — the production's morning newspaper.
Take / setup
One recorded attempt versus one camera-and-lighting configuration.
Rushes / dailies
Yesterday's footage, watched before pride or panic sets in.

Masters to study

Eight filmmakers whose choices still define the grammar everyone else uses.

MasterYearsChangedSteal this
Georges Méliès1861–1938Magician who realized the camera itself was the trick — A Trip to the Moon (1902) invented effects-driven fantasy.Constraints as style: he built whole worlds with a locked camera and paint.
Sergei Eisenstein1898–1948Theorized montage — meaning made in the cut — in Battleship Potemkin (1925).Study the Odessa Steps: length, direction, and collision of shots are the rhetoric.
Orson Welles1915–1985Citizen Kane (1941): deep focus, low angles, and overlapping sound grown-up at once.Let the frame hold competing informations — depth as drama.
Akira Kurosawa1910–1998Weather, movement, and telephoto crowd scenes — Rashomon (1950) gave world cinema the unreliable story.Stage movement through the frame, not just in front of it.
Alfred Hitchcock1899–1980Suspense engineering — the audience knowing more than the characters — across fifty-plus films.Plan in pure sequence: his storyboards were the film; the set was execution.
Agnès Varda1928–2019From Cléo from 5 to 7 (1962) to late digital essays — personal, porous, formally free.The small budget as freedom; the essay film as a way of thinking out loud.
Stanley Kubrick1928–1999Obsessive control in service of unease — symmetry, one-point perspective, the uncanny corridor.Design as psychology: a room's geometry can perform the dread.
Ava DuVernayb. 1972From micro-budget features to Selma (2014) and landmark documentaries — a working model of the modern independent path.Momentum over permission: make the small thing now, the bigger thing follows it.

A short history

WhenEventWhy it mattered
1895Lumière brothers' first public screeningMoving pictures meet a paying audience; the documentary impulse and the train effect are born together.
1902–14Trick films and nickelodeonsCinema learns fantasy (Méliès) and finds its first mass public in storefront theaters.
1927The Jazz SingerSound arrives; cameras gain blimps and booths, and a whole visual grammar survives the transition.
1930s–40sTechnicolor and the studio systemColor, genre factories, and the star machine; also the decade of Kane's deep focus.
1950sWidescreen and the New WavesTV competition widens the frame; young critics (France 1959, then everywhere) pick up cameras.
1970sThe movie-brat blockbuster eraJaws (1975) invents wide-release summer cinema; film schools feed the industry.
1990sDigital VFX and DV camsAnything becomes depictable; cheap cameras put features within reach of credit cards.
2005Video goes platformOnline video turns distribution into a upload button; the creator economy begins.
2010sStreaming and phone cinemaCommissioned binges and a camera in every pocket — more moving images made per day than the twentieth century made per decade.
2020sRemote workflows and AI toolsCloud editing and machine-assisted VFX; the craft debates what authorship means next.

A first year, quarter by quarter

QuarterFocusProjects
Q1Shoot constantly, edit everythingFour one-minute films — a place, a person, a process, an argument; cut each three different ways to feel how order creates meaning.
Q2Sound and sequenceA dialogue scene with full coverage and clean audio; a silent film where sound design carries the story; record room tone everywhere, always.
Q3One real productionA five-minute short with a script, shot list, schedule, and crew of however many you can feed — finished, graded, mixed.
Q4Show it and studySubmit to small festivals or screen for a real audience; watch 52 canonical films with the sound of craft in your ears; direct the next thing better.

Going pro — the paths that actually exist

Film and video is a crew trade with a freelancer's economy on top: almost everyone starts on someone else's set, in a specialty, and branches from there.

PathEntryWhat it becomes
Crew ladderProduction assistant on commercials, indie films, corporate shoots — show up early, solve problems, own a reliable carDepartment climbing: camera assistant, gaffer, sound mixer; union days and rates in film towns
Corporate & event videoWeddings, real-estate video, small-business promos — the steady cash engine of the tradeAn agency or boutique production company; recurring retainers beat one-off gigs
Editor / post specialistCut anything you're given; learn the NLE of your market plus basic color and mixPost houses, unscripted TV, trailer houses; the most sit-down-steady corner of the industry
Documentary & indie festival routeShorts, grants, pitch forums, documentary collectivesFeature financing, festival premiere, broadcast or streamer license — slow, prestigious, precarious
Creator / owned channelA regular published format you can sustain solo — essays, tutorials, seriesAudience-supported production; brand work priced as advertising, not as video

The universal first credit. Every path above starts the same way: be the most useful person on a small set. Rates, rights, and client discipline mirror the photography wing's business page — read it before your first paid shoot; the contracts are nearly identical.

Starter kits, honestly priced

Sound before sensor: the audience forgives soft pictures before inaudible dialogue, so buy the microphone before the camera.

The phone rig

A current phone plus a small gimbal, a lav or shotgun mic with a dongle, and headphones you actually monitor through. Several festival shorts have been shot on exactly this.

The first camera kit

Any mirrorless body with clean video and a fast prime; two batteries more than you think; ND filters for shutter control in daylight — see the filter page.

Light on a budget

One bi-color panel plus a five-in-one reflector and a shower-curtain diffuser; bounce is cheaper than bulbs — the modifier logic transfers unchanged.

The audio kit

Recorder with XLR inputs, a lav, a shotgun with a boom pole made of anything, closed headphones, and a windscreen. Room tone is free; forgetting it is not.

Post bench

DaVinci Resolve's free tier edits, grades, and mixes; any laptop drive-fast enough, plus a second drive for the 3-2-1 backups footage deserves.

The paperwork kit

Call sheet template, release forms, location agreements, and a contacts list — the cheapest items here and the only ones that prevent disasters.
