Strength & Movement — an open studio

The craft where the instrument is your own body: iron, distance, and range of motion trained by principles rather than enthusiasm. This page covers the disciplines, the training principles that govern all of them, the vocabulary, the masters, a short history, a first year, and the professional paths — coaching — that the field supports.

The field in one page

Training is the most measurable craft in this library — kilograms, kilometers, heartbeats — which is both its gift and its trap. The numbers reward consistency and punish enthusiasm equally; the entire field runs on a handful of principles (overload, specificity, recovery) that every good program merely arranges differently. This is the sibling page of The Open Dojo's fitness shelf — the same science, practiced without the gi.

Why people stay

Adaptation is honest and compounding: strength you build at forty carries the groceries at eighty, and the habit — like a garden — outlives the mood that planted it. Few crafts repay boring consistency this generously.

The honest difficulty

The body adapts during rest, not during work, and beginners train exactly as if the opposite were true. The second trap is novelty: program-hopping feels like effort but resets progress every six weeks. Pick a plan, run it longer than feels interesting.

The principles that govern everything

Adaptation's three laws

Progressive overload
The load, volume, or difficulty must creep upward over time; the same stimulus eventually maintains rather than builds. The smallest weekly increase you'll actually sustain beats the heroic one you won't.
Specificity
You get what you train for — heavy slow work builds strength, long easy work builds endurance, and the overlap is smaller than the internet claims.
Recovery is the workout
Stress in the gym, adaptation in bed and at the table. Sleep is a training variable; program it like one.

Technique before load, always

The movement patterns
Squat, hinge, horizontal push, vertical push, horizontal pull, vertical pull, carry — every good program is these seven verbs with numbers attached.
Bracing
Big breath into the belt line, ribs down, abdomen tight — the skill that makes heavy things safe and light things heavier.
Warm-up as rehearsal
Empty-bar grooves, ramping sets of five-three-two — the pattern is taught at every load, not discovered at the top set.
The two-inch rule
Leave two reps in reserve on most sets; true failure is a tool used rarely, not a daily proof of worth.
Film your sets
The same camera discipline as the performing crafts — side-on video exposes what feeling hides.

Programming — arranging the stress

Sets, reps, RPE
The grammar: how many rounds, how many moves, and how hard (Rate of Perceived Exertion) — RPE 8 means two reps left, and honesty about it is trainable.
Frequency over heroics
Three moderate days every week beats one annihilating day followed by six of regret; each pattern touched twice weekly grows fastest.
Deload weeks
Planned lighter weeks — every fourth to eighth — that let adaptation catch up before fatigue files the paperwork.
The runner's 80/20
Eighty percent of mileage easy enough to talk, twenty percent genuinely hard; violating it is the most common way to be slow and injured simultaneously.
Progress runs on cycles
Block the year: base, build, peak, recover. Bodies are seasonal, even if calendars pretend otherwise.

Fuel, monitoring, and not breaking

Protein as scaffolding
Roughly 1.6–2.2 g per kilogram of bodyweight daily supports training — food first, and hydration is part of the same meal.
Pain's two registers
Effort-burn passes; sharp, joint-line, or one-sided pain stops the set. Learning this distinction early is the cheapest education in the field.
Track something
A notebook of loads and runs — the practice log again; what's recorded improves, what's guessed plateaus.
Medical honesty
New training plus existing conditions means a clinician's clearance first; this page teaches craft, not prescriptions.
Consistency beats intensity
The program you'll do in week forty outperforms the optimal one you abandoned in week six. Boring, repeated, compounding — the library's whole theme in iron.

Vocabulary

Rep / set
One complete movement versus a group of them; the atom and the molecule of training.
1RM
One-rep max — the heaviest single you can complete with good form; percentages of it drive programs.
RPE / RIR
Rate of Perceived Exertion and Reps In Reserve — effort dials that autoregulate daily form.
Hypertrophy vs strength
Building muscle size versus expressing force in it — related, not identical, and trained slightly differently.
Compound vs isolation
Many joints at once versus one — the squat versus the curl, workload versus finish work.
DOMS
Delayed-onset muscle soreness — the 48-hour invoice for novel work, not a measure of effectiveness.
Deload
A planned lighter week that lets recovery overtake fatigue.
PR
Personal record; celebrate them, but not every session — PRs are harvests, not crops.
Zone 2
The conversational aerobic effort that builds the engine; unglamorous, decisive.
VO₂max
Maximum oxygen uptake — aerobic ceiling; trainable within genetics' generous limits.
Cadence
Steps per minute in running — around 170–185 for most efficient adults; a cue, not a law.
Mobility vs flexibility
Usable range under control versus passive range — the second without the first is a party trick.
Isometric
Contracting without moving — holds, planks, and the rehab science quietly built on them.
Progression (calisthenics)
The ladder of harder variations toward a skill — incline push-up to push-up to one-arm.
Periodization
Organizing training into blocks so peak effort lands when it counts.
Taper
Reduced volume before an event that keeps intensity sharp while fatigue drains away.

Masters to study

Coaches, athletes, and systematizers whose fingerprints are on every modern program.

MasterYearsChangedSteal this
Eugen Sandow1867–1925Physical culture's first superstar — measured training, physique as art, and the dumbbell book that started an industry.Measure everything: his girth charts were the first training log.
Jack LaLanne1914–2014Brought training to television (from 1951) and lived its principles for nine decades.The long game: consistency across decades is the sport's real event.
Joseph Pilates1883–1967Built Contrology — the method now everywhere — on breath, centering, and controlled range.Treat the midsection as the body's chassis; movement quality is the goal, sweat the byproduct.
B. K. S. Iyengar1918–2014Light on Yoga (1966) codified asana and props; brought precision to Western practice.Alignment as a discipline, props as honesty — the block is a teacher, not a concession.
Bill Bowerman1911–1999University of Oregon coach, co-founder of Nike, and codifier of the hard/easy training day."If you have a body, you are an athlete" — and stress plus rest is the whole formula.
Kathrine Switzerb. 1947Ran Boston 1967 with an official number, was grabbed by an official mid-race, and finished anyway.The sport belongs to whoever shows up; doors pushed open stay open.
Eliud Kipchogeb. 1984Marathon perfection through monastic consistency — Olympic champion twice, and 1:59:41 in a controlled exhibition (2019)."No human is limited," said by the man most famous for doing ordinary things extraordinarily regularly.

A first year, quarter by quarter

QuarterFocusTraining
Q1Patterns & habitThree full-body days weekly — the seven patterns with light loads, filmed sometimes; walking on every rest day; sleep as a scheduled set.
Q2Add load, add distanceSame lifts creeping up by the smallest plates; two easy runs weekly; first 5K event at whatever pace the training gives.
Q3One goal, one blockPick a target — bodyweight pull-up, 10K, first deload-earned PR — and run a dedicated twelve-week block toward it.
Q4The year's harvestTest honestly, deload, then plan next year's blocks from the logbook — the program you'll actually repeat is the one that survived this one.

Going pro — coaching as the career

The field's profession is teaching it: coaching, in gyms, on tracks, in clinics, and increasingly online.

PathEntryWhat it becomes
Personal trainingA recognized certification (NASM/ACE or national equivalent), liability insurance, and floor hours at a commercial gymPrivate client roster or studio share; the trades' apprenticeship model — see client workflow
Strength & conditioningSport-science degree or S&C credential plus volunteer hours with teamsUniversity and professional-team roles; periodization as a full-time job
Endurance coachingRun- or tri-coaching certification, then five athletes trained free for testimonialsClub coaching, plans business, and camps; retention beats recruitment
Rehab-adjacentPhysio/kinesiology routes — the clinical licensesReturn-to-sport and clinical practice; the field's most durable credential wall
Online & content coachingA program you can defend, published consistently, evidence over hypeAudience-scaled coaching and products — the marketing honesty page applies line for line

The field's own safety note

Coaching is a duty of care wearing a lanyard: know your scope, carry insurance, refer clinical issues to clinicians, and program for the person in front of you — not for your own training nostalgia. A good coach's defining skill is keeping people training, injury-free, for decades.

Starter kit

Cheaper than every other page in this library; the best equipment is a schedule.

Shoes, two kinds

Flat, firm soles for lifting (the floor is feedback); cushioned trainers for running. The do-everything shoe does neither well.

Gym access or the home version

A basic barbell gym membership, or home starter: adjustable dumbbells, a doorway bar, and floor space — gear lust hits this field hard; resist.

Bands & a mat

One loop-band set for warm-ups and assisted work; a mat that doesn't slide — the mobility kit in two purchases.

A timer & a watch

Interval timer for work/rest honesty; any watch that records runs. Data you'll actually review, not jewelry.

The logbook

Paper or app — loads, distances, how it felt. The single highest-return object in the field.

Recovery basics

A water bottle, a bedtime, and food you cooked — see the culinary studio; the kitchen is the training partner nobody skips twice.

Cross-training note. Training teaches the library's core lesson with iron clarity: boring consistency, logged honestly, compounding for decades — then every other page in this building is a variation on it.
