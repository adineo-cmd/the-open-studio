Gear & Optics — cameras, lenses, and the kit that earns its place

Gear is the least mysterious part of photography and the most heavily marketed. This page is the counterweight: what each piece actually does, what it costs in money and carrying weight, and what it cannot do for you. No brands here — categories and trade-offs only. How the controls on all this machinery behave is the camera & exposure page's business.

Camera types — the honest comparison

Every camera is a light-tight box with a lens and a sensor. The categories differ in sensor size, lens flexibility, and how much stands between you and the picture — nothing about image quality is as simple as the price tags imply.

TypeStrengthsLimitsSuitsComplexity
SmartphoneAlways with you; computational night and HDR stacking; instant sharingSmall sensor limits low light and depth control; slab ergonomicsEveryone — plus working pros for sketches and social delivery none
Compact (fixed lens)Pocketable with a real sensor, usually one-inch; bright built-in zooms; dials teach manual controlNo lens changes; prices creep toward mirrorless territoryTravelers, street shooters, second-camera owners light
Mirrorless (APS-C / full frame)The current mainstream: the EVF previews exposure, lens ecosystems are deep, stabilization common, burst and video strongBatteries drain faster; menus run deep; good glass is costlySerious learners through most working pros moderate
DSLROptical finder with zero lag; decades of inexpensive used glass; ruggedA declining line; bulky; the finder doesn't preview exposureBudget learners and used-lens bargain hunters moderate
Medium format digitalHuge sensors: tonal smoothness, resolution for wall-size prints, shallow depth from normal lensesBody, glass, and storage costs; slower handling; weightCommercial, studio, fine-art landscape work demanding
Film & rangefinderThirty-six frames teach discipline; a distinct palette; rangefinders are small, quiet, discreetDevelopment and scanning time; no exposure preview; rangefinder focusing is a craft of its ownStudents of the craft, documentary traditionalists unforgiving

Complexity, from none to unforgiving: how much the format asks of you before results feel reliable. Whatever the body, the controls it carries are the same — see the exposure triangle; the words on the dials are in camera parts.

Sensors — size, crop factor, equivalence

Sensor size is the quiet variable behind most of photography's price ladder. Sensors are measured against full frame — 36 by 24 mm, the old 35mm film gate — by crop factor: how much smaller the diagonal is.

SensorApproximate sizeCrop factorWhere it lives
1-inch type13.2 × 8.8 mm2.7×Compacts, drones, camcorders
Micro Four Thirds17.3 × 13 mm2.0×OM System and Panasonic mirrorless
APS-C23.5 × 15.6 mm1.5× (1.6× Canon)Most entry and mid-range mirrorless
Full frame36 × 24 mm1×Professional and flagship bodies
Medium format (4433)43.8 × 32.9 mm0.79×Fujifilm GFX, Hasselblad X

Multiply a focal length by the crop factor for its full-frame equivalent angle of view: a 50mm on APS-C frames like 75mm. The same arithmetic applies to aperture when depth of field is the question — f/2.8 on Micro Four Thirds renders the depth of f/5.6 on full frame at the same framing. That is aperture's other face, and the reason "equivalence" arguments never end.

Low light is about total gathered light: at the same framing and f-number, full frame gathers about four times the light of Micro Four Thirds and holds roughly two stops better in the dark — the ISO 3200 that is clean on full frame is ISO 800 there (see ISO). The arithmetic cuts both ways: a 300mm on APS-C frames like 450mm, which is why wildlife shooters love crop bodies — reach without long-glass weight.

Does sensor size matter? Yes at the margins, less than the marketing says. Big sensors win in dim rooms, huge prints, and razor-thin depth of field — real edges for event, astro, and portrait work. But daylight flattens most differences, computational phones stack ten frames instead of enlarging one, and a skilled photographer with any modern sensor out-pictures a distracted one with medium format. Price the lenses, not the body: the glass outlives it and costs more. The right size is the one whose weight you will actually carry.

Lenses — anatomy of the tube

A lens is a tube of glass with four numbers on it — focal length, maximum aperture, filter size, weight — and a few mechanisms that matter more than the marketing.

Focal length
Millimeters — roughly the distance from optical center to sensor, focused at infinity. It sets the angle of view; zooms cover a range, primes one length. The full map is under focal lengths.
Maximum aperture
The brightest f-number, printed on the barrel. It buys light and shallow depth of field (what f-numbers mean). Zooms often list two — f/3.5–5.6 — because the lens slows as you zoom.
Stabilization
Moving elements in the lens (OIS) or the sensor itself (IBIS) cancel camera shake by a claimed three to six stops. They steady the camera, never the subject — motion blur doesn't care.
Aperture blades
Seven to fifteen of them; more and rounder blades make rounder highlights and smoother bokeh, and stopped-down point lights become sunstars.
Filter thread
The front diameter in millimeters. Lenses that share a size share filters — buy once, use everywhere.
Minimum focus distance
How close you can work, and the magnification there. True macro means 1:1, life-size on the sensor — see the macro genre.
Focus motor
Faster and quieter every generation; video wants silence and no focus breathing. How the camera drives it is in focus.

What focal length does to a scene. Perspective — the size relations between near and far — is decided by where you stand, not by the glass; focal length decides where standing is practical. Walk in close with a wide lens and near things loom while backgrounds retreat; step back with a long lens and the scene's planes stack up, compressed like stage flats. So "wide lenses distort faces" is a misnomer — nose-to-nose distance distorts faces, and wide lenses force you there. See viewpoint and depth.

Focal lengths are named by habit, not decree, but the habits are real. The table runs in full-frame millimeters — divide by your crop factor for the equivalent.

Zooms compress this map into one barrel — a 24–70 covers four rows — while primes stake out one row and usually win on speed, size, and price. That trade is next.

Primes versus zooms — the oldest argument

Both sides are right, which is why the argument never ends. The honest version: primes win on speed, size, and price; zooms win on flexibility and speed of framing.

The prime case

One focal length, no compromises: usually a stop or two faster, smaller, sharper per dollar, and cheaper than the zoom covering the same ground. The cost and the virtue are the same thing — you move. Zooming with your feet teaches framing, and the constraint ends arguments with yourself.

The zoom case

One lens, many framings: zooms keep pace with events, wildlife, and travel, where the shot won't wait for a lens change and an open sensor is a dust magnet. Modern pro zooms are optically superb, and the 70–200 mm f/2.8 has earned its place in nearly every working bag.

100 mm f/2.8 macroShort tele, 1:1Macro, product, food — and portraits; the versatile specialist

Support — tripods, monopods, clamps

The sharpest lens in the world is defeated by a tired arm. Support gear is unglamorous and durable — after the camera, the equipment most likely to still be in your bag in twenty years.

Tripods. Aluminum legs are cheaper, heavier, and cold in winter; carbon fiber is lighter, stiffer per gram, and priced accordingly. Check folded length against travel and maximum load against your heaviest lens. Raise the thickest leg sections first, spread fully, and treat the center column as a last resort — every centimeter up costs stability. Heads are chosen separately: a ball head is the fast everyday choice; a three-way pan-tilt is precise axis by axis; a geared head micro-adjusts each axis for architecture; a gimbal balances long lenses for birds in flight. Whatever the head, the ritual is the same — stabilization to its tripod mode, timer or remote, and a bag hung from the hook when the wind argues.

Monopods, clamps, beanbags. A monopod buys a stop or two and, more importantly, takes a 300 mm off your arms for a whole match — the sports and wildlife compromise between mobility and support. Clamps grip rails, doors, and branches where no tripod stands; a beanbag over a car window or a rock is the safari standard; a table tripod lives in laptop bags forever. The law is the same for all of them: something rigid between camera and world, then a timer. For the body's half of the bargain, see holding the camera.

Filters — three that earn their place, one argument

Filters are glass in front of your glass, and every optical surface costs a little contrast — so each one has to justify itself. Three do easily.

Circular polarizer (CPL). The one effect no software fakes. Rotating it cuts reflections from water, glass, and leaves, darkens blue sky most at ninety degrees from the sun, and restores the saturation haze steals. Cost: one to one and a half stops, some composing patience, and an uneven effect across ultrawide frames.

Neutral density (ND). Sunglasses for the lens — even gray absorption of two, three, six, or ten stops. The point is never darker pictures; it is slower shutters and wider apertures in bright light: waterfalls at a quarter second at noon, f/1.4 portraits in full sun, thirty-second exposures from a 1/30-second frame with a 10-stop filter (see shutter speeds). Buy decent glass — cheap NDs cast color and soften corners — and compose and focus before mounting.

Graduated ND (GND). Dark on top, clear below, blended in the middle — the classic fix for bright skies over dark land. Honest update: bracketing and blending in the editor have replaced most of that work and handle crooked horizons better; the physical filter still wins when the light changes faster than a bracket.

UV, the protection debate. On film, UV filtration cut haze that digital sensors simply don't record, so today's UV filter is pure insurance — a sacrificial pane before an expensive front element. The case for: salt spray, blowing sand, stadium railings, and one careless wipe are real, and filters are cheaper than elements. The case against: an extra air–glass surface adds flare and costs contrast, and a $25 filter on a $1,000 lens is the worst of both. A fair resolution: protected glass in hostile environments, bare glass everywhere else.

Step rings. They tie the kit together: buy filters in your largest thread diameter and step-up rings to share them across smaller lenses — step up, never down, and skip stacking a polarizer plus ND on anything wider than 24 mm; the corners will tell you.

Flash gear — speedlights, triggers, modifiers

A hotshoe flash is the most light per dollar and per pound in photography, and the least used well. The gear is simple; the vocabulary is the barrier.

A speedlight's power is rated by guide number — distance times aperture at a given ISO, so bigger is brighter. TTL meters through the lens and adapts shot to shot: fast, occasionally surprising, wonderful for run-and-gun. Manual repeats exactly: slower to dial in, and the only choice once you light deliberately. High-speed sync pushes the flash past the usual 1/200–1/250 sync limit at a cost of power — daylight portraits wide open become possible.

Triggers turn one flash into a lighting kit. Radio triggers work through walls and around corners — the default; optical slaves fire on seeing another flash and cost almost nothing; an on-camera master can command remotes in TTL. Start with one flash, one trigger, and the ceiling: bounced off white, flash stops looking like flash. The craft side lives in flash lighting and lighting setups.

Storage — cards, drives, and one RAID line

Files are the only output that matters, and they live on the least interesting gear you own — until the day it fails. Buy for reliability, not capacity bragging.

Cards. SD labels decode once and stay decoded: UHS-I and UHS-II describe the bus (the pin layout), while V30, V60, and V90 promise sustained write speeds in MB/s — the number that matters for video and deep bursts. The "up to" read speeds on the box are marketing. CFexpress Type B (and the smaller Type A) outpace SD for high-resolution bursts and RAW video; microSD lives in drones and action cameras. Match the card to what the camera can actually write, buy fewer good cards than many dubious ones, and format in camera, not on the computer.

Drives. The working ritual: copy every shoot to two places the same night — one fast portable SSD for the edit, one drive for the shelf — and let the 3-2-1 rule govern the rest. And the RAID one-liner, since someone will ask: a RAID array protects against a dead disk, not against you — it copies deletions and ransomware as faithfully as photographs. Treat RAID as uptime; the offsite copy is the backup.

Three starter kits — three budgets, no brands

Models change yearly; categories don't. Each kit below is brand-generic on purpose — any maker's current equivalent will do. Buy for the job you have, not the job you imagine.

Under $700: body and one prime

Start here

A refurb or used entry mirrorless — any current APS-C or Micro Four Thirds body — runs roughly $450–600; its native 50 mm-equivalent f/1.8 prime adds $125–200, and a card plus spare battery take the rest. That is the whole kit, and it outshoots every professional rig of twenty years ago. The phone stays in the pocket as the wide and always camera; the prime teaches aperture like nothing else.

Around $2,000: the enthusiast kit

Grow into

Any current mirrorless APS-C or entry full-frame body ($1,000–1,300), a standard zoom — a 24–70 mm f/4 class or the maker's 16–55 mm f/2.8 equivalent — plus the fast 50 if not already owned. Add an aluminum tripod with a ball head, a polarizer in the zoom's thread size, a real bag, and a second battery. This kit needs no replacing for a decade; most of what people outgrow is skill, not gear.

The working pro core

Earn it

Two bodies — redundancy is the job; a camera that dies mid-wedding takes more than pictures with it. 24–70 mm f/2.8 and 70–200 mm f/2.8, two flashes and triggers, cards in pairs mirrored across both slots, six batteries, a weather-sealed bag, insurance, and the discipline to back up the same night. The pro kit is defined by redundancy, not expense. What to charge for it is a different page — finding clients.

Gear doesn't take pictures. Every photograph on the masters page was made with equipment worse than the $700 kit above — slower, dimmer, decades out of date. Gear sets what is easy; attention sets everything else. Before upgrading, name the specific picture you lost for lack of the gear — most itches fail that test. Then go shoot the drills with what you own.
