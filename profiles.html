Portrait

One human, one frame — the oldest commission in photography and still the most direct.

A portrait tells you what meeting a person was like. People love the genre because the camera is an excuse for conversation, because window light and expression repay years of study, and because it is the profession's spine — headshots, families, and commissions all grow from one skill: making a stranger comfortable enough to be looked at.

A short history 1839 → today

Robert Cornelius made one of the earliest photographic portraits in 1839, outside his family's Philadelphia lamp works — an exposure of minutes. In the 1860s Julia Margaret Cameron focused close and soft on friends' faces. Yousuf Karsh's 1941 Winston Churchill set the standard for the public portrait; Richard Avedon's In the American West (1979–1984) left sitters nothing to hide behind but a white backdrop.

A typical shoot

A session is arranged backwards from a mood — formal or loose, window light or one strobe — and tested on yourself before the subject arrives. The first ten minutes are warm-up; keepers come after the subject forgets the camera, which you arrange by talking, adjusting, and showing a good frame when energy dips. Bank the flattering setup early; experiment late; end on time.

The skills that matter

Reading light on a face
The classic patterns are the grammar.
Direction
Words that move a chin two degrees without freezing a smile.
Glass & distance
85mm-class compression; see focal lengths.
Exposure discipline
Skin is the metering subject; meter for it.
Retouch restraint
Local edits, not a new face.

Masters to study

Karsh for gravitas; Cameron for intimacy; Avedon for the empty studio; Annie Leibovitz for portraits built from the subject's own world — several appear on the masters page.

Getting started

A 50mm f/1.8 and one north-facing window will carry a year; white foam board is the first reflector. One friend a week, one lighting setup mastered before any strobe arrives.

Landscape

Scenery with intent — the genre of waiting, returning, and reading light like weather.

Landscape photography is control reduced to position, timing, and lens choice — which is why people love it. Everything in the frame is given; the craft is where to stand, when to press, and what to leave out. It rewards planning and luck equally, and ends in a print.

A short history survey cameras → Group f/64 → dye-transfer color

Carleton Watkins's 1861 Yosemite plates helped push President Lincoln toward the Yosemite Grant of 1864; William Henry Jackson's 1870s Yellowstone photographs fed the case for the first national park in 1872. In 1932 Ansel Adams, Edward Weston, and Imogen Cunningham formed Group f/64, and the Zone System of Adams and Fred Archer, taught around 1940, gave the genre its light-meter arithmetic. Eliot Porter's dye-transfer color in the 1962 Sierra Club book In Wildness Is the Preservation of the World argued that color belonged; digital finished the argument.

A typical shoot

Shoots are scouted at midday on purpose — compositions found, sun angles noted — then shot at the edges of day: tripod up before first light, filters on, hyperfocal distance set, frames bracketed while the light moves fast. The rest is the honest half of the genre: waiting, re-composing, knowing when the show is over. Tide tables and a planned retreat are tools as real as the tripod; return to the same places until they improve you.

The skills that matter

  • Weather and light prediction — the day's arc, read the night before.
  • Composition with depth — foreground, leading lines, a scale cue.
  • Focus discipline — hyperfocal work, stacking when the foreground demands it.
  • Filter craft — polarizer and graduates; see filters.
  • Print finishing — the final act lives in output.

Masters to study

Ansel Adams — see his masters entry — for pre-visualization and print craft; Watkins for what a wet plate could do with a valley; Porter for color as a serious instrument; Michael Kenna for moonlight and long exposure.

Getting started

A kit lens and a used tripod are enough; add a polarizer before any lens, and photograph one nearby scene through a full year of light. Buy the wide zoom once you can name what it would fix — see the kit page.

Street

Unposed life in public space — a genre of timing, nerve, and manners.

Street photography finds pictures rather than makes them: nothing arranged, everything noticed. It is the cheapest genre to start — a phone qualifies — and among the hardest to master: the frame is composed at walking speed and the moment never repeats. Its central tension is ethical — what is fair to take from strangers, and what to keep.

A short history Atget → the Leica → The Americans → Vivian Maier

Eugène Atget spent 1897 to 1927 photographing Paris as plain “documents for artists.” The 1925 Leica made the candid possible, and Henri Cartier-Bresson gave it a theory — the decisive moment, the English title of his 1952 book. Robert Frank’s The Americans (1958) traded elegance for grain. Vivian Maier, a Chicago nanny with tens of thousands of unseen frames, became its posthumous legend after a 2007 auction.

The ethics are not decoration

Laws differ by country, and morals differ from laws. Photograph people as you would want to be photographed, stop when someone objects, and never publish a picture that would reasonably harm its subject. If a frame is for sale, check when a release is required.

A typical shoot

One body, one 28–40mm lens, nothing that rustles. The camera is set before leaving — f/8, 1/250 s, auto-ISO capped, zone focus at a few meters — so a raised camera is a finished decision. Then hours of walking with intent: crossings, markets, pockets of good light, backgrounds worth waiting in. When someone notices, be pleasant and honest; most conversations end in a better picture.

The skills that matter

Zone focus
Distance pre-set, exposure mode chosen so no frame is lost to menus.
Timing
Reading gesture half a second ahead.
Composition at speed
Frames within frames, geometry mid-stride.
Manners
Calm when noticed, graceful when refused.
Selecting
Ruthless culling; see workflow.

Masters to study

Cartier-Bresson for geometry; Frank for nerve; Saul Leiter for color through glass and rain; Maier for proof that attention compounds.

Getting started

Whatever fits in a pocket — the phone counts. Shoot rush hour, assign yourself corners and subjects, and practice the drills on the exercises page. A used 35mm prime comes later, once earned.

Wildlife

Animals on their own terms — fieldcraft with a camera attached.

Wildlife photography looks like a long-lens genre and is actually a naturalist’s genre: the camera work is the last ten percent, and the first ninety is knowing where the animal will be, being there quietly first, and treating its comfort as a hard constraint. People love it for the mornings — out before light, still for hours, rewarded rarely.

A short history Kearton hides → conservation plates → subject-detection autofocus

In the 1890s the British brothers Cherry and Richard Kearton built hides — even a fake ox — to photograph wild birds at their nests, making field natural history popular entertainment. In early-1900s America, William Finley and Herman Bohlman’s bird photographs fed Theodore Roosevelt’s conservation campaigns. Digital burst and mirrorless autofocus have since moved the hit rate toward craft; none of it replaced knowing the animal.

The subject comes first

No picture justifies disturbing an animal: keep distance, never bait predators, avoid nest sites in breeding season, and follow reserve rules without exception. A flushed animal wastes energy it needs — and animals stressed by approach are the dangerous ones.

A typical shoot

Everything decisive happens before the shutter. You research the species — season, feeding hours, likely perch — and arrive before dawn, settled when activity peaks. Long glass rests on a beanbag at a vehicle window; exposure is set for fur or feathers, shutter at 1/1000 s or faster, the background chosen when you picked the position. Then stillness — hours of it, broken by bursts, ending with a quiet withdrawal.

The skills that matter

Naturalist knowledge
Behavior, seasons, and sign — the real teleconverter.
Long-lens technique
Fast shutters, solid support.
Exposure for animals
A white egret and a black raven want opposite metering.
Hide craft
Blinds and vehicles that let you vanish in place.
Patience
Days per keeper, cheerfully.

Masters to study

Frans Lanting for natural-history storytelling; Art Wolfe for behavior as design; Paul Nicklen for polar work; Michael “Nick” Nichols for elephants and lions followed over years.

Getting started

Skip the 600mm: a used 300mm f/4 or 100–400mm-class zoom and a beanbag at a backyard feeder teach more than any exotic trip. Read the lens page before spending; let ethics be your first habit.

Wedding

Documentary pace with formal craft — one day, no retakes, files that must outlive you.

A wedding compresses every people-photography genre into one unrehearsable day: portraits at nine, photojournalism at the vows, event coverage at midnight. People are drawn to that intensity — and to work that matters at the moment people are most aware of being photographed. It is also a business with real stakes, which is why contracts and backups appear below.

A short history 1854 → press cameras → photojournalism’s grammar

Roger Fenton photographed Queen Victoria and Prince Albert in wedding dress in 1854 — fourteen years after the ceremony, which is what technique and convention allowed; for decades couples simply visited a studio. Press cameras and flash after the Second World War moved photography inside the ceremony, usually for a handful of frames. The 1970s and 1980s imported photojournalism’s candid grammar — Denis Reggie became its loudest advocate and covered John F. Kennedy Jr.’s secret 1996 wedding — and digital made coverage continuous.

A typical shoot

The day runs on an agreed timeline: dress and details, preparation of both partners, quiet positioning through the ceremony (venue flash rules honored absolutely), then family formals from a written list, done in twenty-five minutes. Reception work is low-light craft: ISO high, flash bounced or dragged, first dances from the floor’s edge. The invisible half is redundancy — two bodies, dual card slots, everything copied twice the same night per the 3-2-1 rule. The real product is trust, delivered for decades.

The skills that matter

  • Low-light craft — clean high ISO and wide apertures in reception dark.
  • Flash that doesn’t look like flash — bounce and drag until it reads as ambient.
  • Group direction — a list, a caller, cheerful inevitability.
  • Anticipation — reading the day two events ahead of the toast.
  • Business hygiene — contracts and pricing that protect both sides.

Masters to study

Denis Reggie for the documentary wedding’s arguments and best-known frames; Joe Buissink for available-light romance; Jeff Ascough for reportage under real conditions. Notice how little their work resembles a shot list.

Getting started

Second-shoot for an established photographer for a season — the only honest apprenticeship. Two used dual-card bodies, one fast zoom (or 35mm plus 85mm primes), a hotshoe flash, insurance — and never a friend’s wedding as sole photographer until you have watched several.

Macro

Life at 1:1 and beyond, where a millimetre is a landscape.

Macro means magnification, not closeness: at 1:1 the subject is rendered life-size on the sensor, and a few millimeters of focus decide everything. People love it because any yard, season, or weather becomes infinite subjects — frost, pollen, beetles, water beads — and because patience outperforms budget here more than anywhere else.

A short history microscopes → bellows → focus stacking

Close-up work began at the microscope — photomicrography was established laboratory practice in the nineteenth century — then walked outdoors on bellows and extension tubes. The naturalist F. Percy Smith made tiny creatures a sensation in the 1900s; his 1910 film The Acrobatic Fly, a fly juggling matchsticks, proved the small world worth staring at. Digital focus stacking — dozens or hundreds of frames merged into depth no aperture could hold — culminated in Levon Biss’s Microsculpture beetles, assembled from thousands of exposures.

A typical shoot

Insects are best found cold and slow in the first hour of light, while dew still weighs them down. Settings are conservative — f/8 to f/16, shutter at sync speed, a diffused flash doing the real lighting, because ambient never suffices at these magnifications. Brace everything: elbows on knees, lens hood on a fist in the dirt, breath timed between frames. Static subjects — frost, feathers, jewelry — become stacking sessions; wind is the enemy, still mornings the schedule.

The skills that matter

Magnification & working distance
Why 100mm beats 50mm on living subjects; see lenses.
Flash diffusion
Modifiers at insect scale — centimeters away.
Focus stacking
Step size, overlap, and the software that merges it.
Fieldcraft
Approach, wind, dew, patience with things that fly.
Small-scale composition
Background and pattern still rule.

Masters to study

Levon Biss for stacked, monumental insects; Thomas Shahan for jumping spiders with personality on a shoestring; Nicky Bay for Singapore night macro and rare behavior.

Getting started

Extension tubes or a clip-on close-up lens on the kit lens costs lunch money and works immediately. The first real purchase is a used 90–100mm f/2.8 macro — the sharpest money in photography. Build a diffuser from paper and rubber bands.

Astrophotography

Photographing subjects that are dim beyond belief and far beyond sense.

Astrophotography spans several crafts under one name: wide night landscapes with the Milky Way, tracked deep-sky work on nebulae and galaxies, and planetary imaging. What unites them is the arithmetic of photons — everything is faint, so everything is long exposure, stacking, and patience. People love it for the quiet: dark sites, midnight, and a result that feels recovered rather than taken.

A short history 1840 → Vega 1850 → the Orion Nebula 1880

John William Draper photographed the Moon in 1840, a daguerreotype exposed over minutes. In 1850 William Bond and John Adams Whipple recorded the star Vega at Harvard College Observatory — the first photograph of a star other than the Sun. Henry Draper’s 1880 image of the Orion Nebula, exposed for fifty-one minutes, founded deep-sky photography; by the decade’s end Isaac Roberts’s plates had revealed the Andromeda Galaxy’s spiral, invisible to the eye. A century of observatory work followed; the CCD and the consumer sensor moved it all into backyards.

A typical shoot

Planning is half the night: a new or thin moon, a dark site found on a light-pollution map, a forecast trusted only so far. On site, discipline — tripod planted, lens wide open, then precise manual focus on a bright star magnified in live view and locked. Exposures run ten to twenty-five seconds at high ISO before trails set in; shoot dozens of identical frames plus darks for stacking. Cold drains batteries and dew fogs glass — hand warmers are astro kit. The picture is assembled later: stacked, balanced, curves pulled until the nebula surfaces.

The skills that matter

  • Sky planning — moon phase, dark sites, what is overhead this month.
  • Long-exposure craft — shutter discipline, noise averaged away by stacking.
  • Precise focusing — stars are the harshest focus test there is.
  • Post-processing — deep-sky pictures are made in stacking and RAW development.
  • Weather patience — clouds arrive precisely when everything else is perfect.

Masters to study

Wally Pacholka for night landscapes under the Milky Way; Babak Tafreshi, founder of The World at Night project, for sky-and-culture work; Rogelio Bernal Andreo for deep-sky wide-field; David Malin for the observatory plate era.

Getting started

A kit zoom at its widest and fastest already records the Milky Way from a dark site; the essentials are a used tripod and the two-second timer. Add a fast 24mm-class prime when ready, free stacking software before paid, and only then a star tracker — it cannot plan a clear night.

Food

Making food read the way it tastes — half photography, half staging.

Food photography is commercial art with a stylist’s soul: the photographer controls the light, but the subject is engineered — oiled, propped, re-garnished — minutes before the shutter. People love it because a kitchen window serves as an indefinite studio, it rewards prop-hoarders, and one honest picture of a bowl of noodles can carry a restaurant.

A short history still-life painting → Penn’s freezer → window light

The genre inherits composition from the seventeenth-century still-life painters and its trade from the mid-century advertising studio, which made food heroic and faintly impossible — glazed, steaming, built by specialists. Irving Penn’s 1977 Frozen Foods series for Vogue treated groceries as sculpture and loosened the rules. In the late 1990s David Loftus shot Jamie Oliver’s cookbooks in window light with crumbs left in place; the natural style became mainstream, and the 2010s flat-lay finished the job.

A typical shoot

Everything is staged twice — once on the plate, once on the set. The light is a window tamed with a scrim, a white board bouncing it back, a black flag keeping the dark side honest; the styling station sits within reach — tweezers, oil brush, spare garnish, stand-ins to test light while the hero plate is finished. A plated dish lives minutes, so camera, focus, and tether are locked before food appears; process shots (the pour, steam against dark) are planned, not lucky. Then everyone eats the set.

The skills that matter

Light for texture
Hard versus soft decides whether crust reads as crust; see qualities of light.
Angle discipline
Forty-five degrees, overhead, or straight on.
Styling basics
Oil, heat, undercooking, restraint with garnish.
Color harmony
Prop, surface, and food as one palette; see color.
Speed & tethering
Settings locked before the plate lands.

Masters to study

Irving Penn for still-life seriousness applied to groceries; David Loftus for the window-light revolution; Penny De Los Santos for food as culture and gathering rather than product.

Getting started

A 50mm or short macro, the window seat, two sheets of white foam board and one black — a working food studio. Shoot your own breakfast under the same window for a month; learn plating from cooks, and shoot within minutes of cooking.

Product

Objects lit until they look inevitable — the studio genre with zero excuses.

Product photography is the precision end of the craft: the subject never tires or blinks, which removes every excuse and leaves only the lighting. Each material is a small exam, clients need exact deliverables, and the work runs from a mug on a sweep to automotive campaigns. People love it for problems that stay solved.

A short history mail order → large format → e-commerce volume

The mail-order catalogs of the 1890s through the 1920s needed every product pictured alike, and object-on-white became a trade standard before most studios owned a light meter. Mid-century advertising shot large-format transparencies retouched by hand; digital capture and e-commerce turned the craft into volume with consistency, and today product photographers share the bench with 3-D renderers.

A typical shoot

A shoot begins with the brief’s fine print — aspect ratios, marketplace white-background specs, crops, angles — then the set: sweep, flags, surfaces, and lighting by material. Metal wants gradients shaped across the surface; glass wants definition from behind and bright edges; fabric wants the biggest, softest source you own. Every frame is tethered and checked at one hundred percent for dust, alignment, and verticals, because the client will check. The deliverable is a cleaned, export-ready set of variants — the photograph is the pipeline.

The skills that matter

Lighting materials
A reasoned answer for metal, glass, ceramic, fabric; modifiers are vocabulary.
Tethering & tech discipline
Live review, checked alignment, honest histograms.
Batch consistency
Frame two hundred matching frame two.
Retouch
Dust and reflections removed without fingerprints; see detail work.
Estimating
Usage-based pricing — license as much as time.

Masters to study

Hiro, the studio still-life master of 1960s–80s Harper’s Bazaar, for graphic perfection; Irving Penn, whose advertising still lifes matched his portraits’ seriousness; Karl Taylor for splash-and-glass craft; Alex Koloskov for liquid work.

Getting started

A paper sweep, one speedlight (or a window), white foam flags, a 50mm, a tripod, and the two-second timer — the whole starter studio. Practice on materials, not products: one glass, one metal, one fabric object, until each is lit on purpose.

Fashion

Clothing as subject, culture as backdrop — photography with a production budget.

Fashion photography is a team sport at the intersection of portraiture, still life, and graphic design: the garment is the subject, the model gives it motion and attitude, and the picture sells a mood as much as a coat. People are drawn to the collaboration — stylist, hair and makeup, designer, photographer — and to a commercial machine that still makes room for strangeness.

A short history 1911 → Bazaar and Vogue → Swinging London

Edward Steichen’s 1911 photographs of Paul Poiret’s gowns for Art et Décoration are usually called the first modern fashion photographs; two years later Condé Nast hired Baron Adolphe de Meyer as Vogue’s first house photographer. Martin Munkácsi put models in motion in the 1930s, and Louise Dahl-Wolfe shot Harper’s Bazaar from 1936 to 1958. After the war Avedon and Penn set the standard — Avedon’s 1955 Dovima with the Elephants above all — before Swinging London’s Bailey, Donovan, and Duffy, then Newton and Bourdin.

A typical shoot

A shoot starts from a concept deck and moodboard, weeks out. Casting and fittings settle who wears what; location or studio and the lighting plan are agreed with the stylist. On the day the set moves fast — music up, dozens of looks, hundreds of frames per look — while the photographer directs movement in words a model can use: “slower,” “chin down,” “again, but bored.” Selects are reviewed between setups; finals are retouched to publication standard. The best pictures usually arrive right after the plan breaks.

The skills that matter

Directing movement
Words that produce attitude rather than stiffness.
Lighting command
Any mood on demand — studio grammar plus mixed sources.
Production sense
Call sheets, schedules, being easy to work with for twelve hours.
Visual literacy
A century of the genre, so references are chosen, not accidental.
Pacing
Breaking the rules on purpose keeps it alive.

Masters to study

Richard Avedon for motion and white-space drama; Irving Penn for the timeless studio; Louise Dahl-Wolfe for natural light and intelligence; David Bailey for attitude compressed into a frame.

Getting started

Tests, tests, tests — trade pictures for time with new models, stylists, and makeup artists building books of their own. One light and a white wall beats borrowed gear; a small, strong portfolio with a point of view is the asset. Study the magazines, then shoot something they wouldn’t run.

Documentary

Long attention on real subjects — pictures whose captions have to hold up.

Documentary photography commits to real life at real length: access earned slowly, subjects returned to for months or years, and a claim on truth every generation of the genre has argued about. People love it because photography at documentary pace becomes a way of understanding a subject rather than visiting it — and because an honest series outlives every picture made to sell anything.

A short history Riis and Hine → the FSA → Salgado

Jacob Riis’s flash-lit tenement pictures, reproduced in his 1890 How the Other Half Lives, aimed photography at social conditions; Lewis Hine’s child-labor work for the National Child Labor Committee, begun in 1908, fed campaigns that changed labor law. The Farm Security Administration unit (1935–1944) sent Dorothea Lange, Walker Evans, and Gordon Parks across Depression America — Lange’s Migrant Mother (1936) became the era’s face. W. Eugene Smith built the LIFE photo essay with Country Doctor (1948); Robert Frank’s The Americans (1958) made documentary personal; Salgado’s Workers (1993) and Migrations (2000) made it monumental.

Ethics are the genre

No staging, captions with correct names, places, and dates, and an edit that represents what was there rather than what argues better. Subjects give access; the photographer owes accuracy and dignity in return. The editing ethics page covers the rules that still apply.

A typical shoot

Research first — reading, interviews, a written sense of the story, revised constantly. Access is requested plainly and honored: you are a guest until you are a fixture, and the best work happens after the subject stops performing. Days are long and the shutter slow; a small kit keeps you invisible. Captions are written the same day, because a documentary photograph without one is a rumor. Nights are for backing up twice and the slower, honest edit.

The skills that matter

  • Access and trust — the whole genre; everything follows it.
  • Anticipation — reading a room or a shift, ready before the moment.
  • Caption discipline — names, places, dates, recorded same-day.
  • Working unnoticed — small kit, quiet shutter, honest viewpoint.
  • Ethical judgment — under deadline, without a committee.

Masters to study

Dorothea Lange for empathy with structure; W. Eugene Smith for the essay form; Gordon Parks for a career spent crossing lines American society drew for him; Sebastião Salgado for scale and commitment.

Getting started

One body, one 35mm-class lens, and a subject you can revisit for a year — your street, your trade, your grandmother’s kitchen. Write real captions from day one, lay the work out for critique, and pitch local editors when the series has legs.

Sports

Peak action on deadline — anticipation sold as reflexes.

Sports photography looks like reaction and is actually prediction: the photographers with the frames read the game two seconds early, stand where the play will arrive, and have exposure solved before the whistle. It is a credential-and-telephoto genre at the top and a public-bleachers genre everywhere beneath it — one frame, no excuses, and a scoreboard that tells you if you were right.

A short history Muybridge 1878 → WirePhoto → Ali over Liston

Eadweard Muybridge’s 1878 motion study of a galloping horse — a dozen cameras tripped by thread at Palo Alto — settled a wager about hooves and air and founded action photography. Press sports grew with faster film and the Graflex, and wirephoto transmission (the AP WirePhoto began in 1935) put same-day action into newspapers. Neil Leifer’s 1965 photograph of Muhammad Ali standing over Sonny Liston at Lewiston, Maine, remains the genre’s summit. Digital burst and autofocus tracking moved the craft from landing one frame to choosing among hundreds.

A typical shoot

Preparation is positional: credentials and photo positions arranged, team tendencies studied, one or two plays chosen as targets. Two bodies hang ready — a 24–70mm for celebrations, a 300–400mm on a monopod for the field — shutter at 1/1000 s or faster, back-button autofocus tracking. Stadium light flickers, so white balance gets real attention. At the final whistle the second job starts: a tight edit, honest captions, transmission on deadline. A slow-shutter pan is earned luxury.

The skills that matter

Sport literacy
Anticipation is knowledge wearing a lens vest.
Long-lens & panning technique
Smooth follows, intentional blur when it says speed.
Light management
Mixed, flickering sources; ISO courage indoors.
Workflow speed
Edit, caption, and transmit while the bus loads.
Position etiquette
Working with officials and colleagues without being a hazard.

Masters to study

Neil Leifer for the century’s peak frames; Walter Iooss for athletes as personalities across every sport; Bob Martin for technical perfection and multiple World Press Photo sports awards; Heinz Klutmeier for the play inside the play.

Getting started

Youth leagues and local matches need no credential and never say no. A used 70–200mm f/2.8 or a consumer 100–400mm plus a monopod is the honest kit; shoot one sport until you feel the play forming two seconds early — the exercises page builds exactly that.

Where next

Every profile above leans on the same small shelf of fundamentals.

Whichever genre pulled at you, the support work is shared: exposure you can set without looking, composition you can break on purpose, natural light you can predict, a workflow that protects the files, and — if money enters it — the professional paths. The choosing guide and the first-year plan do the rest.
