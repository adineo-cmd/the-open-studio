Going Pro — the business of photography

What changes when the pictures become a product: the paths people actually earn on, portfolios that get hired, first clients, pricing that survives arithmetic, contracts, rights, and the quiet machinery of marketing. None of it is in a camera manual; all of it is part of the job.

The paths people actually take

Almost nobody earns on one line alone. Most working photographers braid two or three of these — a portrait base, a seasonal wedding peak, some teaching to fill the quiet months.

The portfolio

A portfolio is not a trophy shelf — it is an argument that you can do the specific job the client is hiring for. Every image should be work you want more of.

Build it honestly

Personal projects show intent; second-shooting for an established wedding photographer teaches pace and gives usable images with permission; styled shoots fill genre gaps — but disclose the convention in your captions. Ten images with a consistent eye beat sixty with five styles fighting.

Website vs Instagram

The website is home base: your best twenty images, a clear niche page per service, prices or at least a price range, and a contact form that works on a phone. Instagram is the shop window — it proves you're active and reachable, but the algorithm rents your audience. Own the address clients can always find.

First clients & the workflow

First clients come from word of mouth, local businesses you already buy from, and communities you're genuinely part of. Volunteering for a nonprofit you care about is fine; "exposure" for a commercial client is not — pick the genre you can serve and price it.

A workflow that catches dropped ballsinquiry to delivery

  1. Inquiry — reply within a day, even if only to promise a fuller answer tomorrow.
  2. Discovery call — what they need, where, when, budget range; note names and dates.
  3. Written quote — scope, deliverables, price, validity window.
  4. Contract & deposit — nothing is booked until both exist.
  5. The shoot — with backup cards, charged batteries, and a shot list.
  6. Cull & edit — protect delivery date with calendar buffers.
  7. Preview — a handful of finished frames within 48 hours; it buys goodwill and patience.
  8. Gallery delivery — with a written license and print options.
  9. Balance due — invoiced on delivery, terms stated up front.
  10. Follow-up — thank you, ask for a review and a referral at the happiest moment of the project.

Pricing

Charge by day, half-day, hour, image, or package — but anchor every number in your cost of doing business: (desired salary + annual expenses + gear depreciation) ÷ realistic billable days. Shooting days are the visible tip; editing, email, and travel are the berg.

A worked example

Target take-home $50,000, business expenses (insurance, software, gear fund, studio share) $18,000 → $68,000 to recover. A busy solo portrait shooter bills maybe 90 days a year after editing and admin eat the calendar — $68,000 ÷ 90 ≈ $755/day, so quote $800 and stop apologizing. Weddings with second shooters and album labor bill far higher; stock and prints price per use and per edition instead.

Licensing, not selling

A commercial client usually buys usage: where the image appears, for how long, exclusive or not. Bigger usage multiplies the fee. "Work for hire" means the client owns the copyright from the shutter — that transfers the whole asset, and the price should say so. Keep the frame, sell the ride.

The camera doesn't need to eat — you do. Underpricing feels generous for exactly one season, then becomes the local market rate you can't escape. Price for the year you want, not the job you're nervous about.

Contracts

A contract is not distrust — it is the two of you agreeing on the same daydream in writing. Have a local lawyer review your template once; laws differ by jurisdiction.

What a solid one covers

Scope
What is being shot, where, for how long, and who provides what.
Deliverables
Image counts, formats, retouching depth, and the gallery's lifetime.
Timeline
Preview window and final delivery date, with buffer.
Payment & deposit
Amount, schedule, deposit (25–50% books the date), late terms.
Cancellation & rescheduling
What happens to the deposit, weather calls, date changes.
Kill fee
Payment if the client cancels a commercial shoot late.
Image rights
The exact license granted — usage, duration, exclusivity — and what you keep.
Revisions
How many rounds of edits are included before extra billing.
Liability cap
The remedy limit if something goes wrong; insurance backs this up.
Releases
Who is responsible for model and property permissions.

Rights, releases, and the things you never sign away cheap

In most jurisdictions, you own the copyright the moment the shutter fires. Everything after that is licensing — unless you sign it away. Learn the difference before the first client asks.

A model release is permission from a recognizable person to use their likeness commercially — editorial and news use generally needs less, advertising needs more. A property release plays the same role for private property and some landmarks. A usage license grants specific rights for specific purposes; keep every right you don't sell. And your workflow files are yours: RAWs are the negatives of the craft.

Don't give away the farm

"All rights, in perpetuity, worldwide" and unlimited RAW handovers appear in the friendliest-sounding contracts. They are transfers of your entire future income from that work. Charge transfer prices or decline — politely, in writing, once you've checked what the words mean.

Stock

Stock sells licenses to images parked in a library. Microstock pays pennies per download on enormous volume and has been roiled by generated imagery; curated agencies ("macrostock") pay real license fees but are selective about who they take. Treat stock as a residual income stream that compounds over years, never as a launch plan — the first payout reliably takes months. Keyword honestly, shoot gaps rather than crowded subjects, and read the exclusivity terms before uploading anywhere.

Prints

Selling paper is the oldest income in photography and still one of the most durable. A fine-art lab on cotton rag paper justifies gallery prices; a consumer lab suits volume family orders. Decide editions before you print: open editions sell cheap and forever; limited editions must actually be limited — numbered, signed, and honored when they sell out, or the number means nothing. Framing costs double fast at fairs; price the frame or sell unframed. And hang your own work at least once: nothing teaches you what you should have shot like watching strangers walk past it.

Marketing without becoming a brand bot

Marketing is mostly being findable and being referable. A portfolio site with one clear page per genre — "Cleveland family photographer" beats "storytelling of light" — plus a completed business profile listing, covers the people already searching. Local networking does the rest: wedding venues, planners, florists, and chambers of commerce refer the photographers they've met. Post on social as evidence of work, not as a referendum on your worth — recent shoots, behind-the-scenes, the occasional teaching post. And ask for referrals at delivery, the moment clients are happiest; a handwritten thank-you with two business cards outperforms a quarter of ad spend.

Early mistakes, catalogued

Every photographer below is a real person you already know — some of them are you, a season ago.

Underpricing
Sets the market's memory of you; raising later costs more than starting right.
No contract
The scope grows, the date moves, the deposit evaporates — in that order.
Chasing every genre
Being hireable for everything reads as specializing in nothing.
Gear lust
New bodies don't appear in the photos; booked calendars do. See the honest kits.
No backup
One corrupted card without a copy ends a reputation faster than any bad frame. See 3-2-1.
Slow delivery
Promise 48-hour previews and two-week galleries, then beat both.
Free work for commercial clients
Nonprofits you love, yes; profitable businesses, no.
Ignoring usage
An image licensed for a brochure is not licensed for a billboard; bill the difference.

The long game

Photography businesses rarely die from bad pictures — they die from unbilled hours, unbacked files, and unhedged seasons. Braid your income paths, protect your rights, deliver faster than promised, and let the milestones — not the camera releases — mark your progress.
