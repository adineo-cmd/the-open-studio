Editing & Workflow — from card to print

A photograph is made three times: when you frame it, when you develop it, and when someone finally sees it. This page walks the second act — the honest, unglamorous hours between the shoot and the delivery — for people who would rather understand their tools than memorize sliders.

Workflow — import, cull, develop, export

A fixed pipeline is what lets you find last March's pictures and trust that nothing was lost. The stages below never change; only the time you spend in each does. The technical half of the craft — what the camera recorded — lives on camera & exposure.

StageWhat happensTypical timeTip that saves an evening
Import & copyCard to drive, two copies, renamed and cataloged10–20 min per shootCopy, never move; see the 3-2-1 rule before you format anything.
CullRejects and keepers marked; no editing yetHalf the shoot's lengthCull first, edit never before culling — polishing files you will delete is the classic time sink.
DevelopTone, color, local edits on keepers only1–2 min per keeperEdit one reference frame, then sync its settings across similar shots and fine-tune the stragglers.
ExportDeliverable copies rendered from the mastersSeconds per fileSave export presets — web, social, print lab — so the decision is made once, not per photo.

Cull ruthlessly. The viewer never sees what you deleted, but they feel what you kept: two hundred near-duplicates dilute the ten good frames the way a crowded gallery wall dilutes one painting. Judge at full size, not in the grid; a thumbnail flatters nothing and hides soft focus. Keep the strongest version of each moment, not every version — and be honest about the difference between a keeper and a memory. A portfolio, when you get there, wants selects, not survivors; see portfolios.

The first ten edits to make on any photo

Order matters: framing and light before detail, detail before output. Sharpening after cropping, never before.

  1. Straighten and crop to the final framing — horizons level, distractions out.
  2. Set white balance with the eyedropper on something neutral, then adjust by feel.
  3. Set the endpoints — whites up, blacks down — until the histogram just touches the edges.
  4. Set overall exposure so the midtones sit where the scene deserves.
  5. Recover the extremes with the shadows and highlights sliders.
  6. Add contrast gently — a shallow S-curve does more than the contrast slider.
  7. Refine color — vibrance before saturation, casts out in the color mixer.
  8. Make local moves — dodge the subject, burn distractions, gradient a bright sky.
  9. Apply detail — capture sharpening, noise reduction, lens corrections.
  10. Export at the size, color space, and format the destination actually wants.

RAW — the negative, and why edits are nondestructive

A RAW file is the sensor's near-unprocessed reading — twelve to fourteen bits of data per photosite, one color reading per pixel, before anyone has guessed at the rest. The camera's JPEG is that guess: a development someone designed in advance. Shooting RAW hands the darkroom decisions back to you; the mechanics live under RAW vs JPEG, the hardware under sensors.

Nondestructive editing is the quiet revolution. In a RAW converter your edits are never painted onto the pixels — they are stored as a short list of instructions, in the catalog or a small sidecar file, applied fresh every time the image is displayed. The original data sits underneath, untouched, the way a darkroom negative was kept safe while dozens of prints were made from it. Consequences worth knowing:

  • Nothing is ever final. Reopen a file in five years and every slider returns exactly where you left it — the histogram can be re-drawn, the crop reconsidered.
  • Virtual copies let one negative carry several interpretations — color and monochrome, tight and loose — without duplicating the underlying data.
  • The history panel is a complete undo that survives closing the file; the reset button is always one click away.

Defaults worth setting once. A default import preset — lens corrections on, a sensible amount of sharpening, your preferred starting profile — removes a hundred small decisions per shoot. Leave white balance at "as shot" and correct deliberately in the develop step. Attach a metadata template (your name, contact, copyright) on import so ownership travels with every file from birth; why that matters commercially is covered at rights. And make the import step itself the backup step — the card is not empty until copies exist in two places.

Tone — exposure, contrast, and the curve

Tone work is arranging brightness: where the black ends, where the white ends, and what happens between them. The order that works: endpoints first, overall exposure second, the extremes third, contrast last. Keep the histogram open the whole time — it is the only part of the screen that never lies.

Whites and blacks versus shadows and highlights. These four sliders confuse everyone once, then never again. Whites and blacks set the endpoints — where the brightest and darkest data begin and end — so they anchor the whole tonal scale; push them until the histogram just touches the walls and no further. Shadows and highlights work on the regions in between, lifting or recovering without moving the endpoints — that is why highlights can pull a bright sky back from the edge while whites stays put. Hold the Alt or Option key while dragging an endpoint slider and most converters will show exactly which pixels clip.

The curve, explained gently. A tone curve is just a graph: input brightness along the bottom, output brightness up the side, and a diagonal line meaning "change nothing." Click a point and drag it up, and everything near that brightness lifts; drag down, it darkens. Contrast, then, is simply bending the line into a shallow S — brights pushed a little brighter, darks a little darker — while the endpoints stay pinned, so nothing clips. A gentler S is a softer image; a steeper S shouts. The old darkroom papers had personalities described exactly this way, which is why the vocabulary of film survives inside the sliders.

Exposure, the first slider, changes everything at once — in RAW it behaves like re-choosing an ISO after the fact, within the latitude the file allows. It is the loudest tool on the panel, which is not the same as the most useful: an image rescued by endpoints, regions, and curve usually looks more natural than one shoved around by exposure alone.

Color — balance, mixing, and grading

Color work has three layers: making the light's color honest, adjusting individual hues, and choosing a palette on purpose. The eye forgets color faster than any other quality, which is precisely why it is worth doing deliberately. Composition in color — how hues argue and agree — is treated at composition & color.

The white balance tool. Click the eyedropper on anything that should be neutral — a gray card, a white shirt, concrete — and the converter does the arithmetic: a temperature slider in kelvin (blue to orange) and a tint slider (green to magenta). Under one kind of light a click is a complete fix; under mixed light — window plus lamp — no single setting is right, and you choose which truth to tell or fix regions locally. The camera's own attempt lives under white balance.

The HSL panel / color mixer. Eight or so color channels — red, orange, yellow, green, aqua, blue, purple, magenta — each with its own hue, saturation, and luminance controls. This is surgical color: slide orange's luminance down and faces darken without touching the exposure of the whole frame; shift blue's hue and a sky moves from cyan toward violet. Most of a signature look lives here, learned by dragging one channel at a time and watching what changes.

Split toning and color grading. The modern grading panel offers color wheels for shadows, midtones, highlights, and the whole image — tint the shadows teal and the highlights warm and you have half the cinema look. Restraint is the craft: shifts of a few points read as atmosphere, large ones read as Instagram filters from a certain era.

Vibrance versus saturation. Saturation multiplies every color equally — and skin is a color, so skin goes sunburnt first. Vibrance boosts the muted colors hardest and protects the already-saturated ones, which is why the advice is always the same: vibrance first, saturation only if vibrance was not enough.

Local adjustments — painting with light, after the fact

Global sliders treat every pixel alike; real photographs are rarely that fair. Local tools let you brighten the face and not the wall, deepen one sky corner, and quiet one bright window — the digital descendants of the darkroom's most skilled work.

Brush

The freehand tool: paint an effect wherever it is needed — dodge a face, burn a distracting corner, lift a shadow under a hat. Feather the edges, check them at 100 percent, and let the mask overlay show you the truth.

Linear gradient

A smooth falloff across the frame — built for skies that need half a stop of recovery and floors that need darkening. The digital heir of the graduated filters landscape shooters once screwed onto lenses.

Radial gradient

An oval pool of light or dark with soft edges: a gentle brightening on the subject, or the classic "inverted vignette" that draws the eye inward without announcing itself.

Selections that select themselves. Modern converters add one-click masks for subject, sky, background, and even individual people, and let you combine them — "the sky, but only where it meets the mountains, minus the water." Used honestly, they save an hour of brushwork per image; used carelessly, they leave halos along edges. Zoom in and inspect the seams; software is confident in ways pixels are not.

Burning and dodging, the heritage. Every tool on this page has a darkroom ancestor: printers would shape light by hand during the exposure of a print — dodging held light back from areas to be brightened, burning gave extra light to areas to be darkened — and Ansel Adams considered the print itself a performance, remade and refined for decades. The sliders changed; the intention did not: lighten what should draw the eye, darken what should wait. The why of the eye's path is composition's business — see balance.

Detail — sharpening, noise, and the lens's fingerprints

Detail work is the last mile: small moves that read as "quality" without announcing themselves. Do it after the crop and the resizing are decided — sharpening a frame you will later cut is sharpening pixels nobody will see.

Sharpening. A lens and sensor always soften slightly; sharpening adds local contrast along edges to compensate. There are two kinds, and conflating them is the classic error: capture sharpening undoes the sensor's blur once, on the master file; output sharpening is applied at the end, tuned for the destination — screens want less, prints want more, and print size changes the answer. The panel's four controls — amount, radius, detail, and masking — reward one habit: hold Alt or Option while dragging the masking slider and the converter shows you a map of exactly which edges will be touched, leaving smooth areas like skin and sky alone.

Noise reduction. Noise comes in two flavors: luminance (the grain-like speckle) and color (the red-green confetti), and color noise is nearly free to remove. Luminance reduction trades detail for smoothness, so the modern trick is better: AI-based denoising that works from the RAW data itself and rescues files that once were hopeless. Prevention still beats cure — exposing fully to the right, per the histogram, keeps shadows where noise lives quietest; the ISO trade-offs are at ISO.

Clarity, texture, dehaze. Three cousins, three scales of contrast. Clarity boosts contrast in the midtones — a punchiness that flatters rock and ruins skin. Texture works at a finer scale, bringing out surface detail without the halos. Dehaze cuts atmospheric haze, deepening a washed sky and adding punch at the cost of saturation — powerful on distant mountains, heavy-handed anywhere near a face. All three punish greed; a little looks like a better lens, a lot looks like a slider.

Lens corrections. Every lens leaves fingerprints — barrel or pincushion distortion, darkened corners, color fringing wide open. Converters ship with profiles that fix all three in one click, and fixing them before any local work means your gradients are being painted on a straight, even field. The optical causes are explained at lenses & optics.

Software — eight tools, one honest table

Every converter here can develop a RAW file beautifully; they differ in how they organize a library, how they feel under the hand, and what they cost. Choose for the workflow you have, not the one marketing describes — the concepts transfer completely, because the sliders differ but the ideas do not.

Learning curve runs an afternoon to a long apprenticeship — how long until the tool disappears and only the photograph remains. Note the pattern: library-first programs are gentler, pixel editors and module-heavy converters steeper.

A sensible start for a learner: pick any one converter — free is fine — and stay in it for a year. Switching tools teaches menus; staying teaches photography. The vocabulary you will meet in every one of them is collected on the terminology page.

Output — export, color spaces, and formats

Exporting renders a copy of your master at a chosen size, format, and color space — the master never changes. Every export answers three questions: where is it going, how big does it need to be, and who (or what) will display it.

sRGB versus Adobe RGB. A color space is the map that says which colors your numbers mean. sRGB is the smallest and the universal one — every browser, phone, and photo lab assumes it — while Adobe RGB reaches deeper greens and cyans that some printing pipelines can reproduce. Send a wide-gamut file somewhere that expects sRGB and it renders dull, not richer. The practical rule: sRGB for anything that travels; wide gamuts only when a lab or client asks for them by name.

DestinationFormatColor spaceSize & sharpening
Web and socialJPEGsRGBLong edge 2048–4096 px, quality 80–90, screen sharpening

The three formats that matter. JPEG compresses by discarding detail the eye rarely misses — perfect for delivery, poor for repeated re-saving, which degrades a generation each time. PNG is lossless with transparency: the format of screenshots, graphics, and the web, and an inefficient whale for photographs. TIFF is lossless and 16-bit capable — heavy, dependable, the interchange format print labs prefer. The print side of all this — papers, labs, selling — continues at prints.

Resizing. Downscaling is safe and even beneficial — shrinking blends pixels and images look crisper, which is why output sharpening exists. Upscaling invents pixels; interpolation guesses politely, AI upscalers guess convincingly. And resize before the final output sharpening, so the sharpening matches the pixels that will actually be displayed.

Backup — the rule that saves careers

Photography's losses are silent: a card reformatted too soon, a drive that spun once and stopped, a laptop that went into the sea. Every working photographer carries at least one such story. The prevention fits in three numbers.

The 3-2-1 rule

Three copies of every image. On two different kinds of media — say an internal drive and an external disk, not two partitions of the same one. With one copy offsite — a drive at a friend's, a cloud sync, a safe across town — because fire, theft, and floods read no folders. Copy the card the night of the shoot; the card is not blank until two of those copies exist and open.

Cards. Format cards in the camera they shoot in, not on the computer — it keeps the file system consistent. Retire cards that ever misbehave; they fail the way batteries do, suddenly and at the worst time, and no photograph is worth a questionable card. Rotate several rather than wearing one out. The hardware options are compared at storage.

Naming. A file named IMG_4271.JPG is anonymous; a folder named 2026-10-24_harbor-sunrise is findable forever. The pattern that survives decades: date first (it sorts itself), then subject, then a sequence — no spaces, no accented characters, nothing clever. Folders by year, subfolders by shoot; the catalog database is the map, but the folder names are the territory. Choose once, never again — renaming an archive is an afternoon nobody gets back.

Ethics — how far an edit may go

Editing has always been interpretation — every print ever made involved choices. The question is not whether you changed the photograph but what you promised the viewer, and whether you kept it.

Photojournalism's line. News standards — the wire services and major contests — are precise: global tone and color adjustments are permitted, cropping is permitted; adding, removing, or moving content is not; staging news scenes is not. The reasoning is simple: the photograph is testimony, and the viewer's trust is the entire product. Documentary work inherits the same discipline — the genre's obligations are described at documentary & street.

Fine art's latitude. Outside testimony, everything is permitted — composites, painted skies, complete invention — because the promise is different: this is my seeing, not your news. The divide is honesty about which promise is being made. A heavily graded landscape presented as fine art is a point of view; the same file entered as photojournalism is a fraud. History's argument about all of this is older than digital — see philosophy of the medium.

Commercial work lives in between and is governed by the brief: retouch within the limits the client agreed to, deliver what the contract says, and keep the master untouched so any change can be revisited. Expectations are set before the shoot, not after — the mechanics of that conversation are at finding & keeping clients.

Disclosure, contests, and AI

Contests are the strictest arena: most require original RAW files on request, forbid composites and heavy manipulation, and — increasingly — require disclosure of generative AI. Disclose anything a reasonable viewer would want to know about how the image was made; prize committees strip winners over omissions, and reputations travel faster than corrections. The same honesty applies commercially — declare your retouching practice to clients before they discover it. When in doubt, disclose: an edit no one had to be told about rarely needed the telling.
