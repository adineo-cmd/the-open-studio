People & portraits

Every genre here runs on the same engine — a person in front of the lens, a conversation, and light that flatters without lying. Sixteen ways to aim it.

GenreWhat it shootsTypical kit & notes
PortraitOne person, face forward — character, mood, and light doing the talking.An 85mm or 70–200mm lens, window light or one strobe in a softbox; rapport is the real kit.
Environmental portraitPeople inside the places that explain them — workshop, kitchen, studio, cab.A 24–70mm, smaller apertures so the background stays legible, portable flash to balance rooms.
HeadshotShoulders-up pictures for actors, company pages, and conference programs.85–135mm, even shadowless light, plain backdrop, tethered so the client sees selects live.
FamilyGenerations together, usually outdoors, rarely still for long.A 35–70mm zoom, shaded light, a fast shutter for the runners, and more patience than gear.
NewbornSleeping babies in the first fortnight, curled and posed on soft props.A 35–50mm, a warm room, white noise — and a spotter's hand just out of frame for every posed shot.
MaternityExpecting parents, usually near the end of the third trimester.A 50–85mm, flowing fabric, window light, and an unhurried pace.
EngagementCouples before the wedding — relaxed, playful, location-led.A 35mm and an 85mm, golden hour, prompts instead of poses.
WeddingA whole day of ceremony, chaos, and family — unrepeatably.Two bodies, fast zooms, bounced flash, a shot list, and steady nerves.
BoudoirIntimate, tasteful portraits, usually in lingerie, always by consent.Fast primes, bedroom window light, strict privacy, and a written understanding of how images are used.
GlamourPolished beauty imagery — skin, hair, and shine.A beauty dish and reflectors, 85–135mm, and retouching applied with a light hand.
FashionClothing as the subject; the wearer as its canvas.A team — stylist, hair & makeup, model — studio or location, and a tolerance for risk.
EditorialPortraits assigned to illustrate a story or profile in a publication.A strobist kit that fits in a backpack, concepts agreed with an art director in advance.
Self-portraitThe photographer on both sides of the camera at once.A tripod, self-timer or tethering, one window, and a supply of honesty.
PetDogs, cats, horses — family members with short attention spans.Fast shutters (1/500 s and up), ground-level viewpoints, pockets full of treats.
Street portraitStrangers met in public, asked, then posed where they stand.A 35–50mm, a two-minute conversation, natural light, and the grace to accept a no.
Senior & graduationMilestone portraits of final-year students, personality included.A 50–85mm, scouted locations, props that say something true about the person.

Places & structures

Genre after genre about where we live and what we built — most of them tripod genres, all of them weather genres. Twelve ways to stand somewhere and make it matter.

GenreWhat it shootsTypical kit & notes
LandscapeNatural scenery from mountaintops down to hedgerows.Wide zoom, tripod, polarizer, and a habit of returning to the same place in different light.
SeascapeCoastline, surf, and tide — water rendered by shutter speed.Tripod and ND filters, distance from the water on rising tides, and a freshwater rinse for salt-laden gear.
CityscapeSkyline, blocks, and the shapes cities make at scale.A 16–35mm, blue hour, tripod, and patience with traffic and pedestrian flow.
Architecture, exteriorBuildings as objects — form, material, and setting.A tilt-shift lens keeps verticals honest; a polarizer tames glass and skies.
Architecture, interiorRooms and spaces that must read the way they were designed.Wide lens, tripod, bracketed exposures, small lights to lift the corners.
Real estateListings that sell the space honestly and fast.An ultra-wide (10–24mm), HDR or flash-ambient blends, and a next-day turnaround habit.
TravelA sense of place — people, streets, food, and weather together.One body, two zooms or a single 24–105mm, and restraint.
Urban explorationAbandoned buildings and the stories left inside them.A fast wide lens and high-ISO body — with permission first; without it, entry is usually trespass.
Rural & agriculturalFarms, harvests, livestock, and the people who run them.24–70mm and 70–200mm, access arranged in advance, seasons studied.
IndustrialPlants, refineries, machines, and the scale of making things.Permission and safety training (hard hat, boots), wide and telephoto to play the scale.
Night cityscapeStreets and towers after the lights come on.Tripod, f/8, long exposures; the blue-hour window balances sky against lit windows.
NightscapeLandscapes and townscapes by moonlight and streetlight.A sturdy tripod, a high-ISO body, long exposures — blue hour into full night.

Access is part of the craft

Railways, rooftops, and abandoned buildings are photographed beautifully every year — and most are closed to the public without written permission. Trespass photographs can be seized, and derelict structures fail without warning. Get the permit, bring a companion, tell someone where you are; see rights and releases for what happens to pictures once you have them.

Nature & wildlife

The genres of seasons and fieldcraft — where success is mostly knowing where the living thing will be before it gets there. Eleven of them below.

GenreWhat it shootsTypical kit & notes
WildlifeWild animals behaving naturally, undisturbed.400mm-plus glass, fieldcraft, camouflage or a hide, and strict distance ethics.
BirdPerched, feeding, and in-flight birds.500–600mm or a 200–600mm zoom, 1/2000 s for flight, hides and feeders sited with care.
Zoo & captiveAnimals in zoos and sanctuaries — honest about the fences.70–200mm plus a 100–400mm, attention to glass glare and barrier geometry.
MacroLife-size and beyond — the world at 1:1 magnification.A 90–105mm macro, diffused flash, focus stacking, wind-free mornings.
InsectBugs, beetles, and dragonflies in close detail.A 100–180mm macro, early starts while cold insects sit still, a palm-sized diffuser.
BotanicalFlowers, leaves, and whole plants, wild or cultivated.A 90–105mm macro, reflector, overcast skies; botanical gardens serve all seasons.
GardenDesigned plantings and the gardeners behind them.24–70mm plus a macro, the owner's permission, and a spring-through-autumn calendar.
UnderwaterReefs, wrecks, and open water.A housing, strobes (water steals red within meters), dive certification, rinse discipline.
Weather & stormSupercells, lightning, and the sky in a temper.Tripod, 30-second exposures for bolts, remote triggers, storm-spotter training — distance is the safety system.
AutumnFall color at peak — leaf, forest, and slanting light.A polarizer to cut leaf glare, peak-color planning, tolerance for crowds at famous spots.
SafariMegafauna from reserves and protected parks.A 100–500mm zoom, a beanbag over the vehicle rail, park rules, and a guide who reads animals.

Studio & commercial

Pictures with purchase orders attached — measured light, exact deliverables, and the satisfaction of problems that stay solved. Nine ways in.

GenreWhat it shootsTypical kit & notes
Product (tabletop)Objects for sale, lit until they look inevitable.A sweep or light tent, speedlights, 50–100mm, tethering, and lint-free gloves.
FoodDishes and drinks that must taste through the screen.50–100mm, a window with scrims, styling tools, and shoots timed to the minute food holds.
BeverageBottles, pours, foam, and condensation.Flags for gradient highlights on glass, flash duration to freeze splashes, patience with stand-ins.
JewelryRings, watches, and stones at high magnification.A 100mm macro, focus stacking, tents and mirrors for sparkle, tweezers and gloves.
AutomotiveCars and motorcycles — paint, chrome, and stance.24–70mm and 70–200mm, dawn locations or very large soft sources, a polarizer for reflections.
Catalog & e-commerceConsistent, spec-compliant images at volume.A fixed lighting rig, mannequins or flat lays, and a batch workflow that never drifts.
Corporate headshotTeams and executives, consistent across a whole company.A one-light kit, grey or white backdrop, framing locked, about two minutes per person.
StockImages licensed broadly rather than commissioned.Whatever genre you already shoot — metadata and keywording become half the job. See stock licensing.
AdvertisingCampaign imagery for brands and agencies.Crewed productions, storyboards, rental gear, and retouchers on call.

Documentary & street

The genres of attention — finding real life and standing in it long enough to matter. Eight traditions that overlap at the edges and argue about the borders.

GenreWhat it shootsTypical kit & notes
StreetUnposed life in public space.A compact camera or phone, 28–35mm, zone focus, and a working sense of what's fair to take.
DocumentaryReal subjects followed over time, with captions that hold up.One body, one lens, notebooks, and the trust of the people photographed.
PhotojournalismNews photographs made and delivered on deadline.Fast zooms, rugged bodies, caption discipline, and press credentials where the story demands them.
War & conflictArmed conflict — soldiers, civilians, and aftermath.Hostile-environment training, a fixer, armor and insurance, and a clear-eyed acceptance of risk.
SportsAthletes and the moments a season turns on.A 70–200mm f/2.8 and a 300–400mm, monopod, 1/1000 s and faster, credentials.
Events & concertGigs, conferences, and parties — ambient light only, usually.f/1.8–f/2.8 primes, high ISO, the first-three-songs rule at the barrier, earplugs.
Wedding photojournalismWeddings covered candid-first, with no directing.Quiet shutters, available light, a second shooter, and restraint at the emotional peaks.
Social documentaryCommunities and social conditions, committed to over years.A 35mm-style kit, deep research, long-term access, and honest representation.

Artistic

Genre as intention — pictures made for walls, books, and the pleasure of the thing itself. Nine approaches, most of them cheap to try and impossible to finish.

GenreWhat it shootsTypical kit & notes
Fine artPictures made as ends in themselves — for walls, books, and museums.Any camera; intention, editions, and print craft carry the rest. See selling prints.
AbstractForm, color, and gesture detached from the thing photographed.Anything from a phone to a view camera; motion and macro are friendly here.
ConceptualIdea-first pictures, staged to illustrate a thought.Sketches, props, production planning, and often one decisive frame per work.
MinimalistThe fewest elements that still make a picture.Telephoto compression or wide emptiness; negative space is the main material.
Intentional camera movementDeliberate blur — the camera as a brush.ND filters, shutter speeds from 1/4 s to whole seconds, and many frames per keeper.
Double exposureTwo frames sharing a single image.In-camera multiple-exposure modes or layered files; pair a bold silhouette with texture.
InfraredNear-infrared light, where foliage glows white.A converted body or a deep IR filter, sun-drenched scenes, and an eye for lens hot spots.
Black & whiteTonal structure with the color taken away.Any camera; learn to see luminance — the darkroom now lives in post.
Still lifeArranged objects under total control.A window or tabletop lights, 50–100mm, and the patience of a seventeenth-century painter.

Technical & scientific

Genres where the method is half the subject — rigs, mounts, stacks, and instruments. Eleven specialties that turn photography into measurement.

GenreWhat it shootsTypical kit & notes
AstrophotographyThe night sky — stars, the Milky Way, planets.A fast wide lens, a sturdy tripod, stacking software; a tracker unlocks more later.
Deep-skyNebulae, clusters, and galaxies built from faint photons.A telescope on a guided equatorial mount and hours of stacked exposures.
Time-lapseSequences of time compressed into motion.An intervalometer, a locked tripod, and fully manual exposure so frames don't flicker.
HyperlapseTime-lapse that moves through space.Measured steps or a slider, a wide lens, and heavy post stabilization.
PanoramicViews far wider than any lens will hold.Portrait-orientation rows on a pano head, a third of overlap, stitching software.
Aerial & droneThe ground from above.A drone flown inside your country's rules and airspace apps — or an aircraft window and a polarizer.
Focus stackingDepth of field beyond what a single aperture can give.A rail or careful manual focusing plus stacking software; standard practice for macro and product.
MicroscopySpecimens through the microscope.A trinocular microscope, camera adapter, controlled illumination, and clean slides.
ForensicEvidence documented for investigation and court.Scale rulers, cross-lighting, chain of custody, and agency training.
MedicalClinical conditions and procedures, documented.Ring or point lights, macro distances, standard view sets — and strict patient-privacy law.
Photogrammetry & 360Measurable 3-D models and virtual tours.Sixty to eighty percent overlap, turntable or orbit patterns, software to build the mesh.

Counts overlap by design — a wedding photographer is a portraitist at two in the afternoon and a photojournalist at eight. Genres are doorways, not walls.

Choosing your genre

Two honest questions do most of the work — what do you already photograph for free, and what gear, patience, and access do you actually have?

Follow what you shoot on vacation

Look back at your last few trips — the frames you made when nobody was paying you. Faces at long dinners point toward portraits; doorways, markets, and strangers toward street; coastlines and ridgelines toward landscape; the bird you chased with a phone toward wildlife someday. Vacation pictures are appetite speaking plainly. If you come home with fifty photographs of buildings and none of your family, believe the fifty — the places & structures shelf is yours, and architecture rewards study as much as any portrait does.

Match the gear and patience you have

A phone or one modest camera already covers street, food, portrait, and the beginnings of macro; see the starter kit page before adding anything. Wildlife wants long glass and hours of stillness; astro wants a tripod, dark skies, and a tolerance for clouds at midnight; sports wants a fast telephoto and credentials; wedding wants redundant bodies and, more than gear, the temperament to be trusted with an unrehearsable day. Buy the genre you can practice weekly, not the one you can only afford annually.

Try before buying. Rent the lens for a weekend, borrow from a camera club, or shoot the genre on a phone for a month first — the honeymoon frames will tell you more than any review. Every one of the twelve genre profiles ends with honest first-gear advice, and the first-year learning path builds the habits any of these genres will demand. When you do buy, buy used and buy once; the classic money mistakes are almost all made in the first excited year.
