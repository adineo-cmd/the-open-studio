From camera obscura to computational photography

Two centuries of fixing light — how a darkened room became a drawing aid, how chemistry became optics, and how optics became software. The origins, a timeline of what mattered, the film era, color's slow acceptance, digital democratization, the movements, and the ideas people have had about what a photograph is.

Origins — the image before the fix

Photography's physics is ancient; only the chemistry is modern.

The principle of the camera obscura — light through a small hole into a dark room projects an inverted image — was observed in antiquity, but it was the polymath Ibn al-Haytham who described and demonstrated it rigorously around 1021 in his Book of Optics. By the Renaissance the darkened room, and later the lensed box, had become a draftsman's tool: Leonardo da Vinci described the effect in the 1490s, and painters are widely suspected — never proven — of tracing projections. For four hundred years the image was there, live and unsteady, and could not be kept.

Keeping it was a chemical problem. Around 1800 Thomas Wedgwood made silhouettes on silver nitrate paper but could not fix them; they blackened in daylight. The breakthrough came in Burgundy: Nicéphore Niépce coated a pewter plate with bitumen of Judea and, in 1826 or 1827, exposed it from an upstairs window at his estate, Le Gras. View from the Window at Le Gras — an exposure of hours, possibly days — is the earliest known surviving camera photograph, now held at the Harry Ransom Center in Austin, Texas.

Working from the partnership with Niépce (who died in 1833), Louis Daguerre shortened the wait to minutes: the daguerreotype was announced in Paris on 7 January 1839, and that August the French government gave it to the world — the event most histories treat as photography's birth. In the same season, across the Channel, William Henry Fox Talbot announced paper photographs of his own: “photogenic drawings” made by the mid-1830s, and by 1841 the calotype — a paper negative from which any number of prints could be made.

The daguerreotype road

A polished silver plate, one unique image, exquisite detail — and no copies. Portrait studios exploded across the 1840s and 1850s; ordinary people could own a likeness for the first time. But every picture was an object, not a publication.

The calotype road

A paper negative, softer but repeatable. Talbot's negative-positive scheme — one exposure, many prints — is the ancestor of everything from glass plates to this morning's scan. The future belonged to it.

Which was “first”? Niépce made the earliest surviving photograph, Daguerre made photography public, Talbot made it multiple. Histories date the art to 1839 by announcement and to 1826 by artifact — and many early “firsts” were made by people whose pictures did not survive them.

The film era — who got to shoot

Between 1888 and the 1990s, photography went from a profession with chemistry to a hobby anyone could keep in a coat pocket.

Roll film changes the audience from operators to everyone

Wet plates demanded a darkroom attendant; roll film demanded only a finger. The Kodak of 1888 outsourced developing to Rochester, the Brownie of 1900 cut the price to a dollar, and the Instamatic made loading foolproof. By the interwar years photography was a mass habit — family albums, holiday snapshots, school pictures — and the serious amateur, darkroom in the pantry and light meter on a neck strap, became a recognizable social type. Cheap cameras made photography ordinary, which mattered more than making it easy.

The golden age of photojournalism roughly the 1930s to the 1950s

The Leica (1925), faster films, and the flashbulb let reporters photograph life as it ran, and the picture magazines gave the work somewhere to go: Life launched on 23 November 1936 with Margaret Bourke-White's Fort Peck Dam on its cover; Look followed in 1937. The era's wars came home in picture essays — Capa ashore at Omaha Beach, Lange in the migrant camps — and the photographer became a public figure. Television took the evenings in the 1950s and 1960s; Life stopped weekly publication in 1972, the conventional close of the golden age.

Magnum, 1947 photographers keep the negatives

In 1947 Capa, Henri Cartier-Bresson, David Seymour, George Rodger, and William Vandivert founded Magnum Photos as a cooperative owned by its photographers — a direct answer to magazines owning both pictures and rights. Members kept copyright and control of their archives, a model durable enough that the agency still runs that way, and it codified an engaged, author-driven documentary style that spread far beyond its roster.

Color — a sixty-year apprenticeship

Color photography worked long before it was respected.

The Lumière Autochrome (1907) was genuinely practical — a glass plate screened with dyed potato starch — but slow, expensive, and softly rendered. Kodachrome (1935) changed the chemistry: vivid, stable color in a 35mm slide, adopted by magazines and amateurs alike within a generation. National Geographic built an identity on it; family slide shows were lit by it.

Respect took longer. Through the 1960s the art world treated color as commerce's accent — advertising, fashion, travel — and black and white as the serious photographer's language. The turn is usually dated to May 1976, when John Szarkowski mounted William Eggleston's one-person show of dye-transfer prints at the Museum of Modern Art (often described as MoMA's first of color photography), arguing that a snapshot's ordinary subject matter could carry art. Eggleston was not alone: Saul Leiter had photographed New York in color since the late 1940s, and in the 1970s Stephen Shore and Joel Meyerowitz drove large-format color across America, making road signs and diners worthy of eight-by-ten negatives. Within a decade color was the default and black and white the statement — the exact reverse of the world the Zone System was built for. Today the choice is expressive, not technical; see using color deliberately.

Digital — the sensor and the feed

Digital photography did not arrive in 1975, but it decided to in about 2005.

Steven Sasson's toaster-sized prototype at Kodak in 1975 recorded 0.01 megapixels to a cassette tape, and his employer, which made most of its money from film, let the idea wait. The sensors came anyway — CCDs first, then the cheaper CMOS chips now in every phone — and by the early 2010s a sensor out-resolved film for most purposes at most prices. The distinctions dissolved one by one: the Nikon D1 (1999) made digital serviceable for photojournalists, the Canon 5D (2005) made full-frame affordable, and the iPhone (2007) made “did you bring a camera?” obsolete. By the mid-2010s phones were doing the actual image-making computationally — stacking exposures for HDR, synthesizing background blur — so a phone photograph is best understood as a rendering built from many frames rather than a single exposure. Modern sensor design and RAW workflow are the craft end of that pipeline.

The case for “democratization”

More people make more pictures than any decade imagined — on the order of a trillion a year. Movements document themselves; a bystander with a phone can hold power to account; a gifted beginner needs no darkroom and no permission to publish. Whole genres — street, food, astro — gained populations camera clubs could never have reached.

The honest complications

Abundance devalues the single image and rewards the algorithm that ranks it; feeds trained photographers to compose for a thumbnail. Ease of capture has not produced ease of consent — sharing faces at scale raises real questions — and computational rendering quietly moved the photograph from “what the light did” toward “what the software decided.” Democratized distribution is not the same as democratized attention.

Both are true at once. The phone camera is the medium's most democratic instrument and its most surveilled, its most creative and its most disposable. The craft response is the one that never changes: slow down, choose, and print something.

Movements — the arguments photography had with itself

Every few decades, photographers disagree in public about what a photograph should be. The recurring argument: should the camera imitate painting, or find its own honesty?

Pictorialism 1880s–1910s
Soft focus, gum bichromate, picturesque subjects — photography dressed as painting to claim art's status. Institutionalized by the Linked Ring in London (1892) and, at first, Stieglitz's Photo-Secession in New York (1902).
Straight photography & Group f/64 from c. 1915; group founded 1932
The rebuttal: full sharpness, glossy papers, no handwork — the photograph's own virtues, not painting's. Paul Strand made the case; the San Francisco circle Group f/64, including Ansel Adams, Imogen Cunningham, and Edward Weston, made it a manifesto.
FSA documentary tradition 1935–1944
The U.S. Farm Security Administration, under Roy Stryker, sent photographers — Lange, Walker Evans, Gordon Parks, Arthur Rothstein, Russell Lee — to record Depression America. It set the template for funded social documentary, and for the arguments about it.
Street photography 1930s onward
Unposed public life, timed and framed — from Cartier-Bresson through Robert Frank's The Americans (1958) to Winogrand, Friedlander, and Vivian Maier, found only decades later.
New Topographics 1975
The 1975 exhibition “New Topographics: Photographs of a Man-Altered Landscape” at the George Eastman House, Rochester, hung Robert Adams, Lewis Baltz, the Bechers, and Stephen Shore among others: tract houses, parking lots, and warehouses treated deadpan, without pity. Its cool, even-toned look dominates how the built environment is photographed now.
Düsseldorf School from 1976
Bernd Becher's teaching post at the Kunstakademie Düsseldorf, where he and Hilla Becher's typologies of water towers and blast furnaces trained Andreas Gursky, Thomas Struth, Candida Höfer, and Thomas Ruff — monumental, clinical, hugely printed: the photography of systems rather than moments.
Snapshot aesthetic 1960s onward
The deliberate borrowing of the amateur's look — casual framing, on-camera flash, drugstore color — as art's language; Arbus, Winogrand, and Friedlander's 1967 “New Documents” pointed the way, Eggleston and Shore carried it into color, and Nan Goldin and Wolfgang Tillmans made diaristic immediacy a career's spine.

Philosophy — what we think photographs do

Two books anchor the arguments: one distrusting the camera, one undone by a single picture of a mother.

Susan Sontag, On Photography (1977). A set of essays (the first titled “In Plato's Cave”) treating photography as a habit of industrial society: we take pictures to certify experience, she argued, and in doing so subtly refuse it; we collect images as a way of collecting the world — her exact phrase, “To collect photographs is to collect the world.” She worried that the flood of images aestheticizes suffering at a distance, and that a camera in hand feels like engagement while functioning as a shield. Read her as a nagging conscience rather than a manual: photographers who argue with Sontag usually end up taking her point.

Roland Barthes, Camera Lucida (1980). Written in mourning after his mother's death, it is less criticism than a wound examined. Searching her effects, Barthes found one photograph of her as a child — the Winter Garden picture — which he refused to reproduce, because what it gave him it could not give a stranger. From that private test came a lasting vocabulary: the studium, the general, polite interest a photograph invites, and the punctum, the detail that rises out of the frame and pricks you, uninvited. And he named the photograph's stubborn core — the certainty that the thing shown “has been” — its noeme: every photograph is evidence of existence, which is why photographs of the dead are unbearable in a way paintings are not.

Is it art? The question is old and mostly settled, though it was bitter. Baudelaire sneered at photography in the 1859 Salon as art's enemy; the pictorialists answered by imitating etchings; the straight photographers by refusing to imitate anything; museums slowly — MoMA established a photography department only in 1940. Since then photography has become contemporary art's common language, and the interesting question moved from “can it be art?” to “what is this picture doing to me?”

Truth and the frame. A photograph has never been the world — it is always a selection. The frame excludes; the shutter chooses a hundredth of a second; the caption steers; Roger Fenton moved cannonballs at Sebastopol in 1855, Soviet retouchers removed fallen officials, and Capa's Falling Soldier is argued over still. Digital editing made alteration frictionless, and generative AI removes the referent entirely — pictures of things no camera saw. What survives is a discipline rather than a guarantee: photographs always interpret, and the honest photographer treats caption, crop, and edit as part of the truth claim.

Reading photographs in the AI era

Treat provenance as part of the picture: ask who made it, when, and for whom before you trust or share it. If you make composites or AI-assisted images, label them — in documentary work an undisclosed edit is a lie about the world, and the editing ethics page covers where the lines usually sit.
