What this library is for

Written for two readers in particular — though everyone is welcome.

New to photography?

Camera manuals bury the good parts in jargon. Start with how exposure actually works, use the scene table below to leave the house with sane settings, then wander the genre catalog until something grabs you — portraits and streets reward nothing but a phone; wildlife and astro want patience and glass. Skim the terminology page on the way and reviews will start making sense.

Already shooting?

Shoot days are precious; the details are easy to forget. Review the lighting patterns the night before a session, double-check your kit list before you drive, plan a year of deliberate practice with the learning paths, and steal compositional sense from the masters — they worked with worse cameras than yours.

This is a wiki at heart. Photographic knowledge has always been handed from photographer to photographer, in clubs, darkrooms, and comment sections; the library just writes it down. If you spot an error or know something these pages don't, the reference should grow from your corrections too — send notes to the editors and they'll be folded in.

Settings spotlight

Exposure is three dials — aperture, shutter, ISO — and every genre leans on a different one first. Starting points for common scenes:

SceneApertureShutterISOWhy it works
Sunlit portraitf/2.81/500 s100Soft background separation, faces frozen, no harsh light for base ISO to fight.
Landscape at golden hourf/8–f/111/60 s or slower100Edge-to-edge sharpness; the tripod carries the slow shutter.
Indoor sportsf/2.81/800 s3200–6400Freezing motion matters more than a clean file — raise ISO without apology.
Night street, handheldf/21/125 s1600Shutter fast enough to handhold, ISO just high enough to allow it.
Star fields, tripodf/1.8–f/2.810–20 s1600–3200Wide open to gather faint stars before the Earth's rotation trails them.
Backyard birdsf/5.6–f/81/1000 s+AutoShutter first — wings demand it; let the camera find the ISO.

These are starting points, not settings — meter the scene, watch the histogram, and adjust.

Build a small home studio

You don't need a leased studio. A cleared corner and a few pieces of gear carry most of the load between shoots.

A sturdy tripod

The sharpest purchase per dollar in photography. Carbon fiber if you carry it far, aluminum if you don't — but nothing that flexes in a breeze.

One fast prime

A 50mm f/1.8 costs little, focuses fast, and teaches aperture better than any tutorial. Low light stops being a reason to go home.

Five-in-one reflector

The cheapest light source there is: bounce sun or flash, diffuse harsh noon, or flag it off. Folds to the size of a pizza.

Hotshoe flash

More light per dollar than any LED panel. Learn to bounce it off ceilings and walls and it stops looking like flash entirely.

Sensor-cleaning kit

A blower for the field, swabs and solution for home. Dust specks live in every blue sky you've ever shot at f/16.

Cards & a backup drive

Two card slots if your body has them, and copy every shoot to two places the same night. See the 3-2-1 rule.

The reading shelf

A short shelf of books that generations of photographers have worn out. Half craft, half eyes — pair them with shooting, not instead of it.

The CameraAnsel Adams

Volume one of the classic trilogy — still the clearest explanation of what a camera physically does.

Understanding ExposureBryan Peterson

The book that taught a generation the triangle, with a scene-by-scene walk-through for each setting.

The Photographer's EyeMichael Freeman

Composition as design: lines, balance, and tension, diagrammed across hundreds of frames.

The AmericansRobert Frank

83 pictures that redefined what a documentary photograph was allowed to look like.

On PhotographySusan Sontag

The essays everyone argues with — what photographing does to seeing, and to being seen.

Camera LucidaRoland Barthes

A mourner's meditation on photographs of the people we've lost; the source of studium and punctum.

Before you head out with a camera

A camera is a license to look, not a pass to intrude. Ask before photographing people where they'd expect privacy, and get a release if the pictures will be sold. Rules differ everywhere — private property, museums, stations, and borders all have their own — so check before you shoot, and never argue past a refusal. Keep a respectful distance from wildlife: no baiting, no flushing, no nests. And keep one eye on the world while you frame it — roads, cliff edges, and tide tables don't care about the shot. Shoot respectfully, and the library will still be here tomorrow.
