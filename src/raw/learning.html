Learning Paths — a curriculum for the first years

Cameras improve by deliberate practice, not by accident. This page turns the rest of the library into a schedule: a first-year plan with monthly assignments, projects measured in weeks, drills for single skills, a way to critique honestly, and milestones that mark the road from beginner to whatever comes after advanced.

The first year, quarter by quarter

One focus per quarter, one assignment per month. The sequence is deliberate: controls first, seeing second, one genre third, a body of work last. Miss a month; just don't skip the order.

QuarterFocusMonthly assignments
Q1The controls — exposure triangle, metering, focusMonth 1: aperture month — one lens, vary only f, watch the background change. Month 2: shutter month — a fountain, traffic, a ceiling fan. Month 3: ISO month — dim rooms, find your camera's honest noise ceiling.
Q2Seeing — composition and lightMonth 4: one device per week from the composition page. Month 5: light diary — the same subject at eight times of day. Month 6: color month — a week each of complementary, analogous, and monochrome.
Q3One genre, deepMonth 7: choose from the genre profiles and complete four real shoots. Month 8: study three of its masters; copy frames to learn, then break them. Month 9: a mini body of work — ten frames that belong together.
Q4Body of work & critiqueMonth 10: edit ruthlessly — cull three months to twelve keepers. Month 11: build a twelve-print portfolio and sequence it. Month 12: show it — a club, an online critique forum, a small print exchange.

Projects that run themselves

A project is a promise with a deadline. The named ones are famous for a reason; the week-long ones below slot between them.

The named projects

365
A frame a day for a year. Hardest week is the third; the year after, you'll recognize your own eye in it.
52 rolls
One film roll per week — or the digital equivalent: 36-frame days, no chimping, no deleting in camera.
100 Strangers
Portrait a hundred people you don't know. The photography is the easy half; the ethics are the lesson — ask, accept refusals gracefully, and be worth saying yes to.
12 Portraits
Twelve deliberate portraits of people you know, one per month, each with intent written down before the shoot.

Week-long assignmentsany order, one per week

  1. One lens all week — whatever it is.
  2. Same corner, different light, five days running.
  3. Black & white only.
  4. One color only — find it everywhere.
  5. Shadows as the subject, not the byproduct.
  6. Reflections: puddles, glass, eyes.
  7. A frame within a frame, every day.
  8. Hands at work.
  9. Windows from both sides.
  10. Doors — open, closed, half-lit.
  11. Silhouettes at dusk.
  12. One square meter of ground, thoroughly.
  13. Pan everything — motion week.
  14. Freeze everything — 1/2000 week.
  15. Overcast only; learn what soft light forgives.
  16. Neon and night streets.
  17. A market — abundance, hands, transactions.
  18. A stranger's portrait, asked for properly.
  19. A self-portrait that isn't a mirror selfie.
  20. Architecture, looking straight up.
  21. One tree, photographed weekly for a month.
  22. Minimal — one subject, emptied frame.
  23. Busy — fill the frame edge to edge.
  24. Tell one story in exactly three frames.

Drills for single skills

A drill isolates one skill and repeats it past boredom — the way musicians practice scales, not concerts. Pair with the camera exercises from the exposure page.

Bracketing afternoon

One static subject, five exposures one stop apart, on purpose. Read each histogram against what your eye saw; learn where your camera lies and in which direction.

Zone-focus walk

Set 4 m at f/8 on a lens with a scale (or gaffer tape the ring) and walk a market for an hour. Composition only — focus is already solved, so it had better be right.

One-aperture day

f/2 all day, wide or tight, near or far. You learn distance management, background discipline, and how much of your composition depended on depth of field.

Pan-following traffic

1/30 s and follow bicycles, buses, dogs. Ten sharp pans out of a hundred is a good afternoon; the streaked backgrounds are the point.

Manual white-balance morning

Set Kelvin by eye before each shot, then compare against the preset. Calibration for your color sense more than the camera's.

Cull 500 to 10

Take a big old folder and cut it to ten, no mercy, no "maybe" pile. Editing is the skill between shooting and showing; it only sharpens under ration.

Critique, given and taken

The fastest eye-training available is critiquing other people's photographs; the second fastest is being critiqued well. Both start with separating camera-craft — exposure, focus, color — from seeing: subject, moment, arrangement. A photo can be flawless and empty, or technically rough and unforgettable; know which axis a critique is aimed at.

Questions that work on any photograph

  • What is the subject, and how did you know in the first half-second?
  • Does the light serve the subject or just illuminate it?
  • Where does the eye land first, and where does it go after?
  • What would you crop — and what would cropping cost?
  • Would it survive in black & white? If yes, why is it in color?
  • One year from now: keep, or cut?

Where to get it

Camera clubs trade structured critique for attendance; online forums trade volume for kindness — read a forum's culture before posting. Print exchanges are the underrated middle: physical prints, honest margins. And critique others generously and specifically — "the light on the near shoulder is doing the work" teaches you more than "nice shot" teaches them.

Milestones

Not belts — checkpoints. You pass each one on evidence, not attendance.

LevelYou can…
BeginnerExpose deliberately in manual and know why each setting moved; read a histogram at a glance; back up every shoot under 3-2-1; name five composition devices and use them on purpose; put focus exactly where you intended.
IntermediateLight a portrait on purpose using the classic patterns; shoot a full event cleanly and deliver early; cull and develop a take the same day; hold a ten-image set that coheres; quote, contract, and invoice a small job without drama.
AdvancedSustain a project over six months and know when it's finished; exhibit or publish; teach a skill and survive the questions; recognize your own style in old folders; braid two or three income paths that survive a slow season.

Level dots run l-1 (lightest, beginner) to l-5 (darkest, advanced) — same scale as the rest of the library.

One honest rule

Every plan on this page works at any pace — the year can take three. The only real failure mode is shooting without intent twice a month and calling it practice. Ten deliberate frames beat three hundred automatic ones, and the masters kept working exactly this way long after they could have coasted.
