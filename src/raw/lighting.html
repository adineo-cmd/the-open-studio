Light & Lighting — the raw material

Photography writes with light — the word itself says so — and every picture is made from the same substance: hard or soft, from one direction or another, one color or another. This page teaches you to read those qualities anywhere, plan around the sun's day, build the portrait patterns, and shape light on purpose. The recording controls are in camera & exposure.

The four qualities of light

Every light — sun, lamp, flash, window — can be described in four words: how hard, from where, what color, how bright. Name all four before every frame and lighting stops being luck.

Hard and soft — the shadow edge tells you the variable that rules them all

Hard light draws shadow edges you could cut along: the crisp stripe through blinds, the sharp chin shadow at noon. Soft light lets the edge fade through a gradient. One variable decides almost everything: the size of the source relative to the subject. The sun is enormous but so far away it subtends a tiny angle — a small source, so hard; thin cloud makes the whole sky the source, so soft. Bigger source, closer subject, softer light — the rest is detail.

Direction, color, intensity

Front light flattens — texture fades, faces widen. Side light rakes across a subject and pulls out every grain: brick, stucco, skin, plowed fields. Back light rims edges, glows through translucent things — leaves, hair, a glass on a sill — and walks subjects toward silhouette, with the camera tempted to underexpose them (see metering). Top light digs the eye sockets, the reason photographers move people to the edge of shade.

Light has color, measured in kelvin: a candle near 1850 K, tungsten around 2700–3200 K, midday daylight near 5500 K, open shade past 7000 K. The camera neutralizes one source at a time (see white balance); mixed light — a daylight window, a tungsten lamp — defeats any single setting. Intensity matters twice: brightness, and falloff — a lamp near the subject leaves the background dark, the same lamp far away lights the room flat. Whatever the source, underexposed frames get noisy (see ISO).

Practice: photograph the same face beside a window, then two meters from it, and compare the shadow edge and the falloff.

Working with natural light

The sun is a lamp on an unattended dimmer, and nature supplies the modifiers free: windows, clouds, shade, and walls to bounce from.

Window light

A window is a softbox the size of the view. Close to it, light is soft and falls off fast across a face — one cheek bright, the far side sliding into shadow. Sheer curtains diffuse, blinds slice, a pale wall opposite is a fill card, and a north-facing window — in the northern hemisphere — never sees direct sun. Move the subject or the chair rather than waiting for weather: facing the window is flat, forty-five degrees off is a classic portrait, ninety is drama (the named versions at portrait patterns).

Open shade, overcast, and dappled light

Open shade — a building's shadow, a wide awning — swaps the sun for the entire sky: soft, even, and cool; angle the face up toward the openness, since top light still pours in. Full overcast is nature's softbox, a diffuser the size of the horizon that wraps everything, saturates color, and forgives. Dappled sun through leaves is ruinous on a face — bright spots on nose and chin read as clutter no edit removes cleanly — so find continuous shade, or commit completely and make the dappling graphic.

The best light is often the light you're standing in. Golden hour is worth chasing, but the overcast parking lot and the kitchen window at ten in the morning have made more good photographs than the gear catalogues admit. Read the light where you are — hard or soft, from where, what color — and every location becomes a studio.

The sun's day

One lamp, one schedule. The sun changes hardness, height, and color hour by hour, and predictably enough to plan around.

TimeThe lightWhat it suits
Blue hour, dawnSun below the horizon; the sky is the source — dim, even, blueQuiet streets, moody seascapes, tripod long exposures
SunriseVery low, often the clearest air; warm, raking, long shadowsTextured landscapes, mist over water, empty streets
Golden hour, morningLow warm sun, gentle contrast, long soft shadowsPortraits, backlit hair, golden fields — the day's most forgiving hours
Mid-morningSun climbing, light whitening, contrast buildingTravel and documentary; sunlit building fronts
NoonHighest and hardest: short shadows, overhead, glareGraphic architecture and street shadows — or open shade
Golden hour, eveningMorning's light, warmer and dustier; the sun sinking fastSilhouettes, warm portraits, long shadows across squares
Blue hour, eveningThe sky dims toward navy as lamps come onSkylines, dusk interiors — the day's second act
NightNo sun; street lamps, neon, moon and skyglowNeon streets, light trails, astro — see astrophotography

Times shift with season and latitude — golden hour stretches long near the poles and barely exists at the equator. Treat the table as a schedule, not a substitute for looking.

Blue hour is short — twenty to forty minutes in mid-latitudes — and the only time the sky's brightness lands near the brightness of city lights, which is why skylines are shot then. Golden hour forgives: low warm light flatters skin, buries clutter in shadow, and turns dust into atmosphere. The color of the day pairs with flash at mixing light.

The classic portrait patterns

Studio portraiture names five arrangements of the key light, after the shadow each draws on the face — the grammar of the genre. Learn them with a window first.

PatternSetupEffect and mood
RembrandtKey about forty-five degrees off-axis, raised above eye level; a triangle of light on the shadowed cheek, far eye still litPainterly, dignified, characterful — the old-master look
LoopKey nearer the camera and a little lower; the nose shadow loops toward the mouth corner without touching the cheek shadowFlattering and versatile — the event-portrait default
Butterfly (Paramount)Key centered and high; a small symmetrical shadow under the nose, reflector under the chinGlamour — cheekbones lifted, face symmetric; the Hollywood studio portrait
SplitKey at ninety degrees; half the face lit, half in shadowDramatic and hard-edged — character, conflict, moral weight
Broad vs shortAn address for any of them: broad keys the side turned toward camera, short the side turned awayBroad widens a thin face; short slims a full one and deepens the mood

Rembrandt, named for the painter's workshop light: turn the face away from the key and raise it until a small triangle appears on the darker cheek while the shadow-side eye stays lit. Enough light for both eyes, enough shadow for a story.

Loop is one step from Rembrandt: slide the key toward the camera and only a small nose shadow remains, curving toward the mouth. Soft, open, reliable — the pattern to shoot a stranger in.

Butterfly centers the key on the nose's axis, raised high, until the shadow beneath is small and symmetrical; a reflector below fills the jaw. Named for Paramount's studios — hard on deep-set eyes, a gift to strong cheekbones.

Split moves the key to a right angle and half the face goes dark along the center line — the pattern of posters and moral dramas, and the strongest statement of the five.

Broad and short name a choice made within all of them: keying the side of the face turned toward the camera reads wider; keying the turned-away side reads narrower, with more of the frame in shadow — the usual, flattering choice.

Practice: with a window as the key, walk a friend's face through all five in ten minutes — turn the chair, watch the nose shadow, name each before shooting.

Setups — one light to four

Patterns describe where the key sits; setups describe how many lights the picture needs. Most portraits ever made used one or two.

SetupThe arrangementWhat it gives youComplexity
One light, no fillA single key at forty-five degrees and above; shadows fall where they fallSimple, honest, contrasty — a window or one lamp gentle
One light + reflectorKey at forty-five; a white or silver bounce opposite returns fill to the shadow sideThe working default; contrast dialed by reflector distance gentle
Key + fillThe fill — larger, nearer the axis, two to three stops weaker — lifts the shadowsRatios replace luck; repeatable in any room steady hands
Three-pointKey, fill, and a back light rimming shoulders and hair off the backgroundThe subject separates from the room; the classic practiced
Four-light + backgroundA fourth light tones the backdrop, or grades it with a gelControl of the frame's every plane demanding
ClamshellButterfly key above the lens axis; softbox or reflector just below the chinThe beauty standard: even, sculpted, both eyes lit practiced

Complexity runs from an afternoon of practice to a career's worth — how long before the setup feels reliable under pressure. Notice how short the ladder is: most working portraits live on the first two rungs.

The names of the lights. The key is the main light — pattern, exposure, and story. The fill never competes: larger, closer to the axis, set relative to the key in stops. The back or rim light, from behind and above, draws a bright edge that peels the subject off the background; the background light decides how bright the wall sits. And clamshell — key above, fill just below the chin, the lens between — removes the shadows under eyes and jaw: the beauty-editorial standard. Hardware lives at flash gear; the genre's etiquette at portraits.

Practice: build three-point with whatever you own — two lamps and a torch count — and photograph one face at every rung of the table.

Modifiers — shaping the same lamp

A bare bulb is a small, hard, undisciplined source. Every modifier changes its size, its spread, its color, or its presence — in that order of importance.

ModifierWhat it does to the lightThe catch
UmbrellaBounce or shoot-through turns a flash into a big, soft source in secondsLight goes everywhere; spill hits backgrounds and glasses
SoftboxSoftness with fences: internal baffles even it, the front panel sizes it, edges feather the falloffEats a stop of output; quality scales with size
OctaboxA round softbox: natural wrap, round catchlightsSame costs; large ones catch wind on stands
GridA honeycomb that narrows the beam and kills spill — contrast and directionCosts light fast; turns polite sources into spotlights
Beauty dishBetween bare flash and softbox: crisp but flattering, sculpted cheeks, defined catchlightUnforgiving of position; skin texture shows
SnootA tube that squeezes light to a tight spot — accents, hair rims, pools on a backdropOne trick, and a hot center
GelsFilms that recolor flash: CTO warms toward tungsten, CTB cools toward daylight, creative gels color at willEach gel costs light; keep them off hot modeling lamps
Flags and scrimsA flag subtracts — shadow sides, background control; a scrim softens without moving the sourceA black card and baking paper work; both cost setup time

Size relative to the subject still rules: a one-meter octabox three meters away is a hard light, and a bare flash bounced off a ceiling becomes the ceiling — meters wide, and soft. Distance does the work modifiers start, and the cheapest modifier is a wall. Hardware specifics at flash gear.

Practice: one flash, one subject, five arrangements — bare, umbrella, boxed, gridded, gelled — same position each time, and label the set.

Flash — adding light on purpose

Flash is the most controllable light there is: full power or a whisper, any position, matched to the sun or set against it.

On-camera, bounced

Tilt the flash head at a ceiling or the wall behind you and the room becomes the source: soft, directional, and invisible as flash. The classic aim is forty-five degrees up and behind you; a bounce card adds the catchlight that ceiling light leaves dead. Mind the surfaces — a red wall dyes the light red (see mixing) — and save direct flash for documentation: it is the flattest light in photography.

Off-camera basics

One flash on a stand, fired by a trigger or a long cable, outranks any on-camera use: at forty-five degrees and above eye level it sculpts, and every pattern at the table above becomes available anywhere. Work in order — position first, power second. Manual power runs from 1/1 down to 1/128: start at a quarter, judge the frame, adjust by halves.

Sync, HSS, and guide numbers

A focal-plane shutter exposes fully open only below its sync speed — typically 1/200 to 1/250 s; faster than that with ordinary flash and the shutter curtain bands the frame black. High-speed sync (HSS) pulses the flash so any shutter speed works, at the cost of reach. Guide number is the power rating: guide number = distance × f-number, so a flash of GN 36 (meters at ISO 100) lights a subject six meters away at f/6. Halve the distance and the light quadruples — the reason flash portraits leave backgrounds dark.

Dragging the shutter

Set a slow shutter — 1/15 to 1/8 s — fire the flash, and one frame carries two exposures: the flash freezes the subject inside its thousandth of a second while the long shutter drinks the room. At dusk, meter the ambient, drop it a stop or two for mood, and let the flash carry the subject — the blur side of the bargain is at shutter speed. Rear-curtain sync fires the flash last, so trails stream behind the subject.

Practice: at dusk in a lit street, drag the shutter from 1/125 to 1/4 s with the same flash power and watch the ambience move.

Mixing flash with ambient light

Every mixed-light frame is two exposures in one: the ambient, set by aperture, shutter, and ISO; the flash, set by power, distance, and aperture. The systems are independent — that is the whole trick.

Expose for the ambient, then add flash

Meter the room and decide its mood: at full brightness a room reads as daytime, a stop down as evening — usually the better story. Aperture changes both systems; shutter changes only the ambient, because the burst is too brief to notice; ISO changes both; flash power changes only the flash. So: place the flash, set the room's mood with shutter and ISO, then raise power until the subject sits right. Raise ISO for the room, never to rescue the flash (see ISO).

White balance tricks with gels

A flash is daylight-balanced, about 5500 K. In a tungsten room near 3000 K the ungelled subject glows blue against the warm walls. Gel the flash with CTO — color temperature orange — set the camera to tungsten, and everything goes neutral (see white balance). Or refuse to match: daylight white balance, no gel, and the room burns orange behind a cleanly lit subject — the warm-cool split of color as a quality, run as a style. CTB runs the same logic toward the blue of dusk.

Practice: shoot one tungsten room three ways — ungelled, CTO-gelled with tungsten white balance, ungelled with daylight white balance — and compare the stories.

High key and low key

High key and low key are policies about shadows, not brightness settings — one fills them, one feeds them, both built on purpose.

High key — shadows filled

Bright wardrobe, bright background, big soft sources or many of them, fill light everywhere, exposure nudged until the histogram crowds its right edge without clipping (see the histogram). Whites stay white because the shadows are lifted to a whisper; the mood lands airy, clean, optimistic. Harder than it looks: everything bright means everything controlled — background lights, large sources, no stray dark object to drag the meter.

Low key — shadows fed

Dark wardrobe, dark background, one source, and flags — or simply an empty room — keeping light off everything that does not need it. Exposure honors the highlights and lets the rest fall; watch the histogram's left edge so the blacks keep a little detail. The mood lands weighted: mystery, drama, solitude — Rembrandt and split live here. The trap is muddy rather than rich darkness; the difference is one highlight worth protecting.

Both looks are easier to build than to fix: decide the key before the shoot, then dress, background, and light for it.

Practice: photograph the same face high key and low key in one session — same lens, two backgrounds, two policies. The pair teaches more than either alone.

Practice — six exercises in seeing light

Light is learned by watching it change, not by reading tables. Each fits an ordinary week; more structured drills live at learning paths — drills, and the camera-side work at the exposure drills.

The window hour

One subject, one window, twenty-four frames: turn the chair fifteen degrees at a time and watch a face walk through every pattern of the day.

Name that light

Collect twenty portraits from magazines and films; for each, say where the key sat, how big it was, and whether it was gelled — before hunting the answers.

One lamp, one evening

A single light and a reflector, walked in an arc around a still life or a patient face. Two hundred frames of one subject; keep ten.

Follow the sun

Photograph the same corner — of a street, a yard, a room — at every row of the daylight table for one day. The set becomes your almanac.

Drag the shutter

Dusk, a moving subject, a flash on rear-curtain: 1/30 down to 1/2 s, and learn what the ambient does while the flash holds the subject still.

Gel match

One CTO-gelled flash in a tungsten room, shot matched, mismatched, and unfiltered. Label the set — it becomes the reference you carry into real rooms.
