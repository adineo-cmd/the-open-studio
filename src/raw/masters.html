Fourteen photographers who changed how we see

A working canon, not a hall of fame — photographers whose pictures rewired some part of the craft: what a photograph was allowed to be, whom it could show, how it was made. Each entry: who and when, the change made, works with dates, a quote or stated philosophy, and one lesson worth stealing.

Alfred Stieglitz

1864–1946 — born in Hoboken, New Jersey; worked in New York, mostly in rooms he opened himself.

The advocate photography argues its way into art

Stieglitz spent fifty years insisting a photograph could be art, and built the institutions to prove it: the Photo-Secession (1902), the quarterly Camera Work (1903–1917), and the 291 gallery, where photographs hung beside the first American showings of Matisse and Picasso. His own work argued the same case, ending in cloud studies meant as pure emotion. Of The Steerage (1907) he recalled in 1942 seeing shapes first: “a round straw hat; the funnel leaning left, the stairway leaning right.”

Works to study

  • Winter, Fifth Avenue (1893)
  • The Steerage (1907)
  • The Equivalents cloud studies (from 1922)

What to steal

Community is an instrument — a journal, a gallery, and a stubborn argument did more than any single frame. And name a scene's shapes before its subject.

Ansel Adams

1902–1984 — born in San Francisco; formed by Yosemite, which he photographed for over sixty years.

Landscape Zone System Black & white

What he changed

Adams made exposure teachable: with Fred Archer he devised the Zone System in the late 1930s — meter, pre-visualize the print, place every tone on purpose. A co-founder of Group f/64 in 1932, he championed the straight, fully detailed print; his Sierra Nevada pictures served landscape photography and conservation at once.

Works to study

  • Monolith, the Face of Half Dome (1927)
  • Moonrise, Hernandez, New Mexico (1941)
  • The Tetons and the Snake River (1942) — on the Voyager golden records, 1977

What to steal

Decide the print before you press: meter what must hold detail and place tones on purpose. The Zone System lives on in every histogram you read.

“The negative is the equivalent of the composer's score, and the print the performance” — hence a lifetime of reprinting Moonrise.

Henri Cartier-Bresson

1908–2004 — born in Chanteloup-en-Brie, France; trained as a painter, then rarely stayed home.

The decisive moment geometry at the speed of life

Cartier-Bresson turned the Leica into a sketchbook and the street into a studio: one 50mm lens, quiet, fast work, almost no cropping — the frame was decided at the shutter or not at all. In 1947 he co-founded Magnum with Capa, David Seymour, George Rodger, and William Vandivert, so photographers could own their pictures. “To me, photography is the simultaneous recognition, in a fraction of a second, of the significance of an event as well as of a precise organization of forms,” he wrote in The Decisive Moment (1952).

Works to study

  • Behind the Gare Saint-Lazare (1932)
  • Hyères, France (1932)
  • Sunday on the Banks of the Marne (1938)

What to steal

Find the geometry, then wait for life to walk into it. One lens and no cropping is the fastest street school there is.

Dorothea Lange

1895–1965 — born in Hoboken, New Jersey; childhood polio left her with a limp and, she believed, an outsider's alertness.

What she changed

Lange gave American documentary photography its conscience. Hired by the Farm Security Administration in 1935, she photographed Depression migration with the plainness of evidence and the closeness of sympathy; in 1942 she documented the internment of Japanese Americans, work the federal government impounded for the war. In 1952 she helped found Aperture.

Works to study

  • White Angel Breadline, San Francisco (1933)
  • Migrant Mother, Nipomo, California (1936) — made in about ten minutes
  • The internment series (1942)

What to steal

Work the situation, not just the face: she talked before lifting the camera, and her captions carry names and places — a documentary photograph owes its subject accuracy and dignity.

“The camera is an instrument that teaches people how to see without a camera.”

Robert Capa

1913–1954 — born Endre Friedmann in Budapest; remade himself in Paris; killed by a landmine in Indochina, May 1954.

Close enough war photography at personal range

Capa defined the war photographer as a participant. On D-Day, 6 June 1944, he waded ashore with the first wave at Omaha Beach; all but eleven frames of his four rolls were ruined in a processing accident, and those eleven remain the visual memory of the morning. In 1947 he co-founded Magnum and served as its first president. “If your pictures aren't good enough, you're not close enough” — though his closeness meant trust and time, not just distance.

Works to study

  • The Falling Soldier, Spain (1936) — long debated, never resolved
  • D-Day, Omaha Beach (1944) — the eleven frames
  • His last roll, Thai Binh, Indochina (1954)

What to steal

Presence over perfection: earn access, stay until the story is yours, and accept that the honest frame may be grainy and unrepeatable.

Margaret Bourke-White

1904–1971 — born in the Bronx, New York; industrial Cleveland first, then Moscow, then the front lines.

What she changed

Bourke-White treated access as the art. She photographed steel mills from catwalks, Soviet industry in 1930 as one of the first foreigners allowed in, and in 1936 joined Life as a founding staff photographer — her Fort Peck Dam made the magazine's first cover that November. The first woman accredited as a U.S. war correspondent, she was present at Buchenwald's liberation in 1945.

Works to study

  • Fort Peck Dam (1936)
  • Buchenwald after liberation (1945)
  • Gandhi at his spinning wheel (1946); she photographed him again on the day he was assassinated, 30 January 1948

What to steal

Get the position nobody else has, and be technically ready when you do. She left no famous aphorism — her creed was preparation, permits, nerve, and heavy cameras up ladders.

Imogen Cunningham

1883–1976 — born in Portland, Oregon; chemistry student, platinum printer, seventy years behind the camera.

Attention, intensified the close look as a style

Cunningham studied chemistry at the University of Washington and printed in Edward S. Curtis's Seattle studio before opening her own. In the 1920s her botanical close-ups — flowers read as sculpture — helped pull photography toward modernism; in 1932 she helped found Group f/64. No movement held her for long: portraits, nudes, street work, and a recumbent self-portrait in her mid-eighties. “Which of my photographs is my favorite? The one I'm going to take tomorrow.”

Works to study

  • Magnolia Blossom (1925)
  • Two Callas (1929)
  • Phoenix Recumbent (1968)

What to steal

Take one subject and stay weeks — a plant, a hand, a face — until it stops being familiar. The cheapest close-up course there is; it never retires: she worked into her nineties.

Richard Avedon

1923–2004 — born in New York; Harper's Bazaar, then Vogue, then The New Yorker.

Fashion Portraits 8×10 view camera

What he changed

Avedon set fashion in motion — an evening gown between circus elephants — then stripped the portrait to white seamless and an 8×10 view camera, where expression had nowhere to hide. For In the American West (1979–84, for the Amon Carter Museum) he gave drifters, miners, and waitresses the scale museums reserved for the famous.

Works to study

  • Dovima with Elephants, Paris (1955)
  • The In the American West portraits (1979–84)
  • Ronald Fischer, Beekeeper (1981)

What to steal

Remove the alibis: plain background, even light, a long sitting — and keep shooting between the poses, where the portrait often arrives.

“My portraits are more about me than they are about the people I photograph.”

Irving Penn

1917–2009 — born in Plainfield, New Jersey; more than sixty years at Vogue, from 1943.

Subtraction as method the studio as laboratory

Penn believed the studio could be as true as the street if you removed everything that flatters. In 1948 he posed celebrities in a corner built of two studio flats, and the corner did the directing: nowhere to stand, nothing to lean on, only posture and face. Tradespeople with their tools (Small Trades, 1950–51) and cigarette butts printed huge (Cigarettes, 1972) got the same severity. He left no aphorism — the method was the philosophy: subtract until only the subject's own arrangement remains.

Works to study

  • The Corner Portraits (1948)
  • Small Trades (1950–51)
  • Cigarettes (1972)

What to steal

Build a corner of your own — one wall, one window, one bare bulb. Constraints direct subjects better than instructions do.

Diane Arbus

1923–1971 — born in New York, and nearly all of her work was made there; she died by her own hand at forty-eight.

Portraits Direct flash New York

What she changed

Arbus photographed people at the margins — performers, twins, giants, Sunday families — frontally and usually with consent, the gaze returned to the camera. In 1967 John Szarkowski hung her work at MoMA in “New Documents” with Winogrand and Friedlander, reframing documentary as a personal act; after her death she became the first photographer shown at the Venice Biennale (1972).

Works to study

  • Child with a Toy Hand Grenade in Central Park (1962)
  • Identical Twins, Roselle, New Jersey (1967)
  • A Jewish Giant at Home with His Parents in the Bronx (1970)

What to steal

Slow the encounter down: meet, talk, return. Her directness came from consent and time, not stealth.

“A photograph is a secret about a secret. The more it tells you the less you know.”

Vivian Maier

1926–2009 — born in New York, raised partly in France; a nanny on Chicago's North Shore for some forty years.

The secret body of work no audience, no edits, no explanation

Maier is the century's great argument that practice, not audience, makes the photographer. On her days off she walked Chicago and New York with a Rolleiflex — children, straphangers, her own shadow — and showed the work to no one. In 2007 a Chicago auction house sold a locker of her negatives; researcher John Maloof bought part of it for a few hundred dollars and began reconstructing an archive now exceeding a hundred thousand images. She had died in April 2009, before he could reach her. She left almost no words about photography — the philosophy is in the work: look, daily, whether or not anyone is watching.

Works to study

  • The Rolleiflex self-portraits (1950s–70s)
  • Chicago and New York street scenes (1950s–80s)
  • Her later color work (1970s–90s)

What to steal

Permission to practice without applause: shoot weekly, on a routine nobody rewards, and let the archive be the point — a habit worth drilling.

Sebastião Salgado

Born 1944 in Aimorés, Minas Gerais, Brazil; trained as an economist, based in Paris.

What he changed

Salgado brought epic scale and long time to social documentary. After Magnum (1979–94) he and his wife Lélia Wanick Salgado founded Amazonas Images, spending years inside single subjects: labor, migration, the pre-industrial planet. Since the 1990s their Instituto Terra has replanted his family's exhausted ranch in Aimorés with millions of native seedlings.

Works to study

  • The Serra Pelada gold mine, Brazil (1986)
  • Workers (published 1993)
  • Genesis (photographed 2004–11)

What to steal

Choose a subject that deserves years. His stated conviction: long, unhurried engagement — returning, waiting, being known — lets a photograph carry dignity instead of surprise.

Annie Leibovitz

Born 2 October 1949 in Waterbury, Connecticut; a student in San Francisco, then New York.

The narrative portrait celebrity photography as staging

Leibovitz joined Rolling Stone in 1970 while still a student — her first assignment was John Lennon — and became its chief photographer three years later, at twenty-three; Vanity Fair followed in 1983. She made the staged conceptual portrait mainstream: ideas from the sitter's own life, produced like theater. On 8 December 1980 she photographed Lennon curled naked around a clothed Yoko Ono; he was killed hours later, and the picture became Rolling Stone's January 1981 cover. “When I say I want to photograph someone, what it really means is that I'd like to know them,” she wrote in Annie Leibovitz at Work (2008).

Works to study

  • John Lennon and Yoko Ono, New York (8 December 1980)
  • Whoopi Goldberg in a bath of milk (1984)
  • Demi Moore, pregnant — Vanity Fair cover (1991)

What to steal

Research beats improvisation: build the idea from who the person is, keep it simple enough to shoot in one sitting — and stay past the planned frame. The Lennon picture was the last of the day.

Steve McCurry

Born 1950 in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania; a Magnum member since 1986.

What he changed

McCurry carried vivid color reportage through four decades of conflict and ordinary life, mostly in Asia. He crossed into Afghanistan with guerrilla fighters before the Soviet invasion at the end of 1979, film sewn into his clothes. His portrait of a refugee schoolgirl — Afghan Girl — became National Geographic's June 1985 cover; the sitter, Sharbat Gula, was found again in 2002.

Works to study

  • Afghan Girl, Nasir Bagh camp, Pakistan (1984)
  • Sharbat Gula found again, Afghanistan (2002)
  • The final roll of Kodachrome (2010), given him by Kodak before the film and its last lab closed

What to steal

“I look for the unguarded moment,” he says — wait past the smile for the half-second when the face stops performing. And let one color discipline become a signature, as Kodachrome's warm reds became his.

How to study a master

Fourteen entries is a reading list, not a syllabus. Two habits make any of them useful:

One picture, slow

Take a single frame and sit with it for ten minutes. Name the light, the distance, the moment — you will learn more than from skimming a monograph.

Copy the method, not the frame

Rebuild the conditions — Penn's corner, Lange's captions, Cartier-Bresson's one lens — with your own subjects. Method transfers; pictures belong to their moment.

A note on the canon. Any list of fourteen is an argument, chosen for influence and teachability; whole continents are underrepresented. Read these entries alongside the movements that shaped them and the genres they worked in — and treat the gaps as an invitation.
