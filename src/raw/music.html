Music — an open studio

The craft of organized sound: rhythm you can feel, harmony you can steer, and the long apprenticeship of hands, breath, and ears. This page gives music what the photography wing has — the crafts inside the field, the technique groups, the vocabulary, the masters, a short history, and an honest first year.

The field in one page

Music rewards the same things photography does: daily repetition with attention, an ear that notices more each year, and a habit of finishing things. The instruments differ; the shape of learning — vocabulary, techniques, masters, syllabus — is the one this whole library is built on.

Why people stay

Progress is audible. The song you couldn't play in March sits under your hands by June; the mix that sounded fine last year now sounds unfinished. Few crafts give feedback this honest, this often.

The honest difficulty

The first months are the worst: your ear hears what your hands can't do yet. Everyone quits inside that gap or through it — the only reliable difference is a teacher, a schedule, and music you actually want to play. See how critique works in any field.

Technique groups

Whatever the instrument, these four blocks carry most of the first years.

Fundamentals & deliberate practice

Slow practice
Below the speed you can just barely manage, not at it — errors rehearsed slowly stop being rehearsed at all.
Metronome work
Time is a skill, not a gift. Practice with the click, then against it (accent displaced by one beat) to test whether you own the pulse or borrow it.
Chunking
Four bars, hands separate, joined only when each part is boring. The unit of learning is the chunk, never the whole piece.
Posture & hands
Tension is the tax on bad form. Shoulders down, wrists neutral, instrument adjusted to you — not you to it.
Repertoire ladder
Always one piece at the edge of ability, one comfortable, one easy for joy. All-edge repertoires stall; all-easy ones bore.

Ear & theory, in order

Interval singing
Sing intervals before naming them — the voice trains the ear faster than the ear trains itself.
Transcription
Lifting solos and chord changes by ear, one lift per week. Slow the recording, loop two seconds, find it on the instrument.
Functional harmony
Roman numerals over letter names: knowing a song is a I–IV–V tells you a thousand other songs are too.
Rhythm internalization
Clap, count, and play the same line at three tempos; subdividing ("1-e-and-a") keeps sixteenths honest.

Making records

Gain staging
Healthy levels at every stage, clipping nowhere — the unglamorous habit that separates clean recordings from loud mush.
Mix moves
Cut before you boost; solo less than you think; mix at conversation volume and check in the car.
Arrangement
Production's real skill: what enters when, what drops out, and why the chorus lands the second time.
Reference tracks
Mix against a record you admire in the same style — calibrates your room, your ears, and your ambition.

Playing with people

Locking in
Listen past yourself — to the drummer's kick, the bassist's choice of note length. Bands are built from ears, not chops.
Dynamics
Rehearse quiet/loud contrast on purpose; the audience feels the shape of a set more than any single solo.
Recording yourself
The cheapest honest mirror in music. One phone recording of rehearsal exposes more than a month of assumptions.

Vocabulary

The words that make lessons, tutorials, and liner notes legible.

Pitch
How high or low a note is; the alphabet of music.
Interval
The distance between two pitches — the atom of melody and harmony.
Chord
Three or more pitches sounding as one unit.
Scale
An ordered set of pitches a tradition agrees to use; major, minor, and the modes.
Key
The scale a piece treats as home; "in C" means C organizes the gravity.
Meter & time signature
How beats group and subdivide — 4/4 four beats per bar, 3/4 the waltz's lilt.
Tempo
Speed, in beats per minute or Italian moods (allegro, andante).
Dynamics
Loudness as expression: piano soft, forte loud, crescendo the journey between.
Timbre
The tone color that makes a note on trumpet a different word than the same note on flute.
Melody vs harmony
The tune against its supporting chords — sentence and paragraph.
Riff & lick
A repeated signature figure (riff) versus a one-off improvised line (lick).
Verse / chorus / bridge
The load-bearing walls of song form.
Groove & pocket
When rhythm section timing feels inevitable; playing "in the pocket" is living there.
EQ
Cutting or boosting frequency bands — the mixing desk's tone controls, used surgically.
Compression
Automatically turning down the loud parts so the quiet ones can be heard; everywhere, and easy to overdo.
Mix vs master
Blending tracked parts into a stereo whole, then preparing that whole for release loudness and formats.

Masters to study

Eight musicians and bodies of work that changed how everything after them sounds — and what a learner can steal from each.

MasterYearsChangedSteal this
Johann Sebastian Bach1685–1750Wrote the textbook of tonal harmony — the Well-Tempered Clavier visits every key, twice.Study counterpoint lightly: play one line per hand and hear how independent melodies agree.
Ludwig van Beethoven1770–1827Turned the symphony into an argument; rewrote music's idea of what a piece could say.Motivic development — build whole movements from four notes, as the Fifth does from the start.
Louis Armstrong1901–1971Invented the jazz soloist's job; rhythm and phrasing freedom from New Orleans outward.Phrasing behind the beat and singing through the horn — space is part of the line.
Billie Holiday1915–1959Treated the melody as a suggestion; re-composed songs in the singing of them.Lag the phrase, mean the word — interpretation over volume.
Miles Davis1926–1991Fronted a new kind of band in every decade — bop, cool, modal (Kind of Blue, 1959), fusion.Restraint and personnel: what you leave out, and who you play with, are the decisions.
The Beatles1960–1970Made the recording studio an instrument and the three-minute song an art form.Study the B-sides and outtakes — how ordinary material becomes records through arrangement.
Nina Simone1933–2003Fused classical technique, jazz feel, and protest into one unrepeatable piano-voice instrument.Genre is a decision, not a cage; the cross-training is the style.
Quincy Jonesb. 1933Arranger-producer across jazz, film, and pop — most famously Michael Jackson's Thriller (1982).Arrangement thinking: audition parts for the song, not for the player.

A short history

WhenEventWhy it mattered
c. 1025Guido of Arezzo's staff notationMusic became writable — melodies could outlive memories and travel without travelers.
1600sOpera and the Baroque systemHarmony got rules and drama got music; tonality as we know it took shape.
1721–22Bach's Well-Tempered ClavierShowed equal-ish temperament worked in every key — and taught harmony for three centuries.
1877Edison's phonographSound could be kept. Performance stopped dying with the performance.
1920sRadio and the 78 rpm eraOne performance, millions of listeners; jazz and blues went national and global.
1948The long-playing recordMinutes became sides; the album as a work, not just a hit plus filler.
1950s–60sRock and roll, the studio eraYouth market, electric instruments, multitrack tape — the modern band format was born.
1982The compact discDigital audio entered homes; clarity over warmth became the argument that never ended.
1999–2001File sharing, then the iPodMusic unbundled from albums and objects; a thousand songs in everyone's pocket.
2010sStreaming and bedroom productionDistribution went free and studios went laptop; more music is made by more people than ever.

A first year, quarter by quarter

The same shape as the photography curriculum: controls, then ears, then ensemble, then finished work.

QuarterFocusAssignments
Q1Instrument basicsFirst chords or five-finger positions; two songs you love, learned to the end; metronome at 60 bpm daily for ten minutes.
Q2Rhythm & earOne transcription per month; sing intervals in the car; clap and count everything before playing it.
Q3Playing with peopleJoin anything — jam night, choir, church band, community orchestra. Weekly, not monthly.
Q4Record & performRecord four songs at home, mix them against references, and play one set for humans however small the room.

Starter kits, honestly priced

Buy the cheapest thing that stays in tune and upgrade only when the instrument — not boredom — is the limit. The photography wing's kit philosophy is identical.

Guitar kit

Used acoustic that holds tune, extra-light strings, clip-on tuner, capo. Total can stay under the cost of two lessons — spend the difference on the lessons.

Keys kit

61-key touch-sensitive keyboard, sustain pedal if it accepts one, headphones. Weighted keys are the first real upgrade, not the first purchase.

Voice kit

A recorder for playback, a keyboard or pitch app, water. Lessons matter more here than gear — the instrument can't be bought.

Production kit

Two-input interface, one honest mic, closed headphones, any free DAW. One mic, one room, many months — constraint is the teacher.

DJ kit

Entry controller (software bundled), headphones you can cue in, and a legal way to own the music you play. Record every practice set.

The shared kit

Metronome, music stand, and a practice log — date, minutes, what broke, what clicked. The log is the actual growth hormone.

Cross-training note. Musicians practice scales the way photographers drill exposure; both fields run on finished pieces over perfect ones. Wander back through the photography wing and steal the structure — it translates.
